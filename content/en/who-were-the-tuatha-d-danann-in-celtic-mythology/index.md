The Tuatha Dé Danann were the supernatural divine race of Irish mythology, representing the gods and goddesses of the ancient Irish Celtic pantheon. According to medieval Irish tradition, they ruled Ireland before being displaced by mortal humans, after which they retreated to the Otherworld beneath the earth in sidhe mounds.

## Key Facts

- The Tuatha Dé Danann learned magical arts in four northern cities—Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias—from which they brought four treasures to Ireland.
- They defeated the Fir Bolg at the first Battle of Mag Tuired and later fought the Fomorians at the second Battle of Mag Tuired.
- After losing sovereignty to the Milesians (sons of Míl), they went into the hills and sidhe mounds, becoming the supernatural beings of the Otherworld.
- Key figures included the Dagda, Nuadu Argatlám, and Lugh, though sources are limited regarding other claimed members like Brigid and the Morrígan.
- They were believed capable of reincarnation, with divine personages able to return to mortal existence multiple times.

## Q&A Sections

### What does the name "Tuatha Dé Danann" mean?
The name literally means "People of the goddess Danu" or "Tribe of the goddess". In some medieval texts, the name appears simply as "Tuatha Dé," meaning "People of the god/goddess," with Danann representing the genitive form of the divine name Danu. This connects them directly to Celtic divine tradition as the people or descendants of a primary goddess figure.

### What were the four treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann?
The Tuatha Dé Danann brought four magical treasures from their northern cities: the Stone of Fál from Falias, which roared under every rightful king of Ireland; Lugh's invincible spear from Gorias; Nuadu's irresistible sword from Findias; and the Dagda's cauldron from Murias, which never left any company unthankful. These treasures were taught to them by four wizards—Mór-fesae, Esras, Uscias, and Semias.

### How did the Tuatha Dé Danann lose control of Ireland?
The Tuatha Dé Danann ruled Ireland after defeating the Fir Bolg but were eventually conquered by the Milesians, reportedly the sons of Míl, who represented the Gaelic settlers. According to the medieval invasion scheme, the Milesians were the final and successful wave of settlers who established mortal human rule over Ireland. After this defeat, the Tuatha Dé Danann withdrew from the visible world.

### What happened to the Tuatha Dé Danann after their defeat?
Following their loss to the Milesians, the Tuatha Dé Danann "went into hills and the tumuli [síd-brugaib], so that the síd-beings under the earth granted hostages to them". They became the supernatural inhabitants of the Otherworld, dwelling in sidhe mounds and continuing to influence mortal affairs. They also left five men in each province of Ireland to create ongoing conflicts among the sons of Míl.

### Could members of the Tuatha Dé Danann be reborn as mortals?
Yes, there was a widespread belief that divine personages and members of the Tuatha Dé Danann could be reincarnated as mortals. As one authority notes, "all the chief personages of this cycle were regarded as the direct descendants, or it would be more correct to say, as avatars or reincarnations of the early gods". Their pedigrees were traced back to the Tuatha Dé Danann, indicating their divine origins even when appearing in mortal form.

## In-Depth Overview

### Origins and Learning in the Northern Isles

The Tuatha Dé Danann originated in the mystical northern isles of the world, where they mastered supernatural knowledge. According to the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, "The Tuatha Dé Danonn were in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom". This education took place in four cities: Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias, each associated with specific magical treasures and wizard-teachers.

### Conquest and Rule of Ireland

The Tuatha Dé Danann's arrival in Ireland marked a pivotal moment in mythological history. They first encountered and defeated the Fir Bolg at the first Battle of Mag Tuired, where "a hundred thousand of the Fir Bolg fell". After this victory, "they took the kingship of Ireland". Their rule was characterized by the presence of their magical treasures, particularly the Stone of Fál at Tara, which served as a test of legitimate kingship.

### Internal Governance and Leadership

The Tuatha Dé Danann maintained complex political structures during their rule. After the Battle of Taillten, their chiefs gathered to select a single king rather than remain "divided as we are, serving various kings throughout Erinn". The candidates for sovereignty included "Bodhbh Dearg, son of the Daghdha; and Ilbhreach of Eas-Ruaidh; and Lir of Sidh Fionnachaidh; and Midhir the Proud of Bri Leith; and Aenghus Og, the son of the Daghdha". Ultimately, Bodhbh Dearg was chosen as king "for three reasons, namely: for the sake of his father; for his own sake".

### The War Against the Fomorians

The most significant conflict of their reign was the war against the Fomorians, described in the Second Battle of Mag Tuired. This conflict represented "a version of the Indo-European myth of the war of gods". The struggle was complicated by intermarriage between the two groups, creating "a web of relationships linking individuals of the Túatha Dé Danann with those of the Fomoiri". Notable examples include Balor giving his daughter Ethne to Cian of the Tuatha Dé Danann.

### Transition to the Otherworld

The end of Tuatha Dé Danann rule came with the arrival of the Milesians, but their transition to Otherworld existence was carefully negotiated. They established themselves as supernatural powers dwelling in sidhe mounds, where they continued to interact with mortals. The text describes how "Bodhbh Dearg and the Tuatha Dé Danann they came to the shore of Loch Dairbhreach, and they took up an encampment there, listening to the music of the swans", indicating their ongoing presence in the landscape.

### Continuing Influence and Reincarnation

Even after withdrawing from direct rule, the Tuatha Dé Danann maintained significant influence over Ireland. They strategically placed agents throughout the land: "They left five men for each province in Ireland making a proliferation of battles, conflicts, strife, and aggression amongst the sons of MU". Additionally, their divine nature allowed for reincarnation, with "divine personages, national heroes who are members of the Tuatha De Danann or Sidhe race, and great men, can be reincarnated".

## Primary Sources

***Cath Maige Tuired*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> The Tuatha Dé Danonn were in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom. There were four cities in which they were learning lore and science and diabolic arts, to wit Falias and Gorias, Murias and Findias. Out of Falias was brought the Stone of Fál, which was in Tara. It used to roar under every king that would take the realm of Ireland.

***Aided Chloinne Lir*** *(c. 11th century)*
*transl. in: O'Curry, Eugene, rev. O'Duffy, Richard J., Gaelic Union (1883)*
> Of the history of the Tuatha Dé Danann from the battle of Taillten down: They congregated from all parts of the five provinces of Erinn until they were in one assembly, and in one place of council. And the chiefs of the Tuatha Dé Danann said: 'It is better for us,' said they, 'to have one king over us, than to be divided as we are, serving various kings throughout Erinn.'

***Lebor Gabála Érenn*** *(c. 12th century)*
*transl. in: Koch & Carey, The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> It is the Tuatha De Donann who brought with them the Great Fil which was in Tara, namely, the stone of knowledge after which Ireland is called Mag Fail. The one beneath whom it shrieked used to be king of Ireland, until Cu Chulainn split it; for it did not shriek beneath him or his foster son.

***Mesca Ulad*** *(c. 12th century)*
*transl. in: Koch & Carey, The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> The Tuach De Danann went into hills and the tumuli [síd-brugaib], so that the síd-beings under the earth granted hostages to them [as a token of submission]. [The Tuach De] left five men of their people for each province in Ireland making a proliferation of battles, conflicts, strife, and aggression amongst the sons of MU.

***Aided Chloinne Lir*** *(c. 11th century)*
*transl. in: O'Curry, Eugene, rev. O'Duffy, Richard J., Gaelic Union (1883)*
> And the Tuatha Dé Danann were at that time consuming the Feast of Age in each Sioth in turn; and when they went to Sioth Lir, these four were their joy and their delight, for the beauty and symmetry of their form; and where they constantly slept was in beds in front of their father.

***Aided Chloinne Lir*** *(c. 11th century)*
*transl. in: O'Curry, Eugene, rev. O'Duffy, Richard J., Gaelic Union (1883)*
> As for Bodhbh Dearg and the Tuatha Dé Danann they came to the shore of Loch Dairbhreach, and they took up an encampment there, listening to the music of the swans. And as for the Milesian Clanns, too, no less did they come from every point of Erinn that they might take up an encampment at Loch Dairbhreach in like manner.

***De Gabáil in t-Síde*** *(c. 12th century)*
*transl. in: Koch & Carey, The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> There was a wondrous king over the Tuatha De in Ireland, Dagan by name [a.k.a. 'the Dagda']. Great was his power, even over the sons of Mil after they had seized the land. For the Tuatha De blighted the grain and milk of the sons of Mil.

***Acallam na Senórach*** *(c. 1200)*
*transl. in: Dooley & Roe, Oxford University Press (1999)*
> The Tuatha De Danann are an invading group who settled Ireland before the sons of Mil, the Gaelic settlers. Sons of Mil: the Goedelic settlers of Ireland who, according to the Irish literati's account of the prehistory of Ireland, succeeded the Tiiatha De Danann and wrested control from them.
