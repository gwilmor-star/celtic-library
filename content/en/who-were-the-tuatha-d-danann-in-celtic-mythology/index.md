The Tuath Dé (also spelled Tuatha Dé Danann) were the supernatural race of deities and divine beings in Irish mythology, described in medieval texts as the immortal gods who ruled Ireland before the arrival of the Milesians.

## Key Facts
- The name "Tuath Dé" means "Tribe(s) of the gods" and first appears clearly in the verse of Eochaid ua Flainn (†1004).
- They learned "lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning" in four northern cities: Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias.
- The Tuath Dé brought four magical treasures to Ireland: the Stone of Fál from Falias, Lugh's spear from Gorias, Nuada's sword from Findias, and the Dagda's cauldron from Murias.
- After their defeat by the Milesians, they "went into hills and the tumuli" and became the síd-beings dwelling under the earth.
- They ruled Ireland under kings including Nuada Argatlám and later Bodhbh Dearg, son of the Dagda.

## Q&A Sections
### What does the name "Tuatha Dé Danann" mean?
The term evolved from earlier designations like "Fir Dé" (Men of the gods) and "Tuath Dé" (Tribe of the gods). The full form "Tuatha Dé Donann" first appears in the work of Eochaid ua Flainn (†1004), who is "the earliest source for the eponymous 'Donann, mother of the gods'". However, this Donann figure appears to be a later invention, as the name behaves grammatically like it derives from the group name rather than vice versa.

### Where did the Tuath Dé come from originally?
According to Cath Maige Tuired, "the Tuatha Dé Danann were in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom". They studied in four cities - Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias - each under a wizard teacher, before coming to Ireland.

### What happened to the Tuath Dé after the Milesians conquered Ireland?
After their defeat, the Tuath Dé "went into hills and the tumuli, so that the síd-beings under the earth granted hostages to them". They became the supernatural inhabitants of the otherworld, dwelling in síd-mounds throughout Ireland. They also "left five men of their people for each province in Ireland making a proliferation of battles, conflicts, strife, and aggression amongst the sons of Míl".

### What were their relationships with other supernatural races?
The Tuath Dé had complex relationships with the Fomorians, sometimes as enemies, sometimes as allies through intermarriage. "The Tuatha Dé made an alliance with the Fomorians", and many figures had mixed parentage, such as Lugh, whose father Cían was of the Tuath Dé while his mother Eithne was daughter of the Fomorian Balor.

## In-Depth Overview
### Origins and Learning
The Tuath Dé are presented as a divine race with supernatural knowledge acquired in otherworldly realms. The Second Battle of Mag Tuired describes how they dwelt "in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom". Their education took place in four mystical cities, each associated with a particular wizard-teacher: "Mór-fesae was in Falias: Esras was in Gorias: Uscias was in Findias: Semias was in Murias. Those are the four poets of whom the Tuatha Dé learnt lore and science".

### The Four Treasures
From their four cities of learning, the Tuath Dé brought four magical objects that became central to Irish sovereignty and warfare. "Out of Falias was brought the Stone of Fál, which was in Tara. It used to roar under every king that would take the realm of Ireland". The other treasures were equally potent: "Out of Gorias was brought the Spear that Lugh had. No battle was ever won against it or him who held it in his hand. Out of Findias was brought the Sword of Nuada. When it was drawn from its deadly sheath, no one ever escaped from it, and it was irresistible". Finally, "Out of Murias was brought the Dagdae's Caldron. No company ever went from it unthankful".

### Political Structure and Kingship
The Tuath Dé maintained sophisticated political institutions with elected kingship and formal assemblies. After the battle of Tailtiu, "the chiefs of the Tuatha Dé Danann said: 'It is better for us,' said they, 'to have one king over us, than to be divided as we are, serving various kings throughout Erinn'". The succession process involved multiple candidates, including "Bodhbh Dearg, son of the Daghdha; and Ilbhreach of Eas-Ruaidh; and Lir of Sidh Fionnachaidh; and Midhir the Proud of Bri Leith; and Aenghus Og, the son of the Daghdha". Ultimately, "they came to the conclusion to give the sovereignty to Bodhbh Dearg, son of the Daghdha, for three reasons, namely: for the sake of his father; for his own sake; and on account" of his qualifications.

### Transition to the Otherworld
Following their defeat by the Milesians, the Tuath Dé underwent a transformation from earthly rulers to otherworldly beings. "The Tuach De Danann went into hills and the tumuli, so that the síd-beings under the earth granted hostages to them as a token of submission". This transition established them as the supernatural inhabitants of Ireland's sacred landscape, dwelling within síd-mounds and maintaining influence over mortal affairs. They continued to interact with human society, as evidenced in tales where "Bodhbh Dearg and the Tuatha Dé Danann they came to the shore of Loch Dairbhreach, and they took up an encampment there, listening to the music of the swans".

## Primary Sources

***Aided Chloinne Lir (The Fate of the Children of Lir)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: O'Curry, Eugene (1883)*
> Of the history of the Tuatha Dé Danann from the battle of Taillten down: They congregated from all parts of the five provinces of Erinn until they were in one assembly, and in one place of council. And the chiefs of the Tuatha Dé Danann said: 'It is better for us,' said they, 'to have one king over us, than to be divided as we are, serving various kings throughout Erinn.'

***Cath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley (1891)*
> The Tuatha Dé Danonn were in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom. There were four cities in which they were learning lore and science and diabolic arts, to wit Falias and Gorias, Murias and Findias.

***Cath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley (1891)*
> Out of Falias was brought the Stone of Fál, which was in Tara. It used to roar under every king that would take the realm of Ireland. Out of Gorias was brought the Spear that Lugh had. No battle was ever won against it or him who held it in his hand.

***Lebor Gabála Érenn (Book of Invasions)*** *(c. 11th century)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John (2003)*
> It is the Tuatha De Donann who brought with them the Great Fil which was in Tara, namely, the stone of knowledge after which Ireland is called Mag Fail. The one beneath whom it shrieked used to be king of Ireland, until Cu Chulainn split it.

***Lebor Gabála Érenn (Book of Invasions)*** *(c. 11th century)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John (2003)*
> Nuadu Argatlam, then, was king of the Tuath De Donann for seven years before coming to Ireland, until his arm was cut off in the first battle of Mag Tuired.

*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John (2003)*
> The Tuach De Danann went into hills and the tumuli [síd-brugaib], so that the síd-beings under the earth granted hostages to them [as a token of submission].

*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John (2003)*
> There was a wondrous king over the Tuatha De in Ireland, Dagan by name [a.k.a. 'the Dagda']. Great was his power, even over the sons of Mil after they had seized the land.

***Tochmarc Étaíne (The Wooing of Étaín)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Bergin, Osborn & Best, Richard Irvine (1938)*
> After a time Fuamnach came on a visit to Midir, and along with her as sureties came the three gods of Dana, namely Lug and the Dagda, and Ogma.

***Aided Chloinne Lir (The Fate of the Children of Lir)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: O'Curry, Eugene (1883)*
> And the Tuatha Dé Danann were at that time consuming the Feast of Age in each Sioth in turn; and when they went to Sioth Lir, these four were their joy and their delight, for the beauty and symmetry of their form.
