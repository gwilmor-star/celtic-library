Celtic mythology is the collection of stories, beliefs, and gods that come down to us from the ancient Celtic-speaking peoples of Ireland, Britain, and mainland Europe. The Iron Age Celts never kept a written record of their own, so nearly everything we have was written down later by Christian scribes in medieval Ireland and Wales, centuries after the world that made these myths had changed beyond recognition. What we're left with is still the real thing, old and rich and strange, but it's also been filtered, reshaped, and at times deliberately covered over.

## How do we know about Celtic mythology if the Celts didn't write it down?

We know about Celtic mythology almost entirely at second hand: through manuscripts copied out by Christian scribes in medieval Ireland and Wales, through Roman-era inscriptions, and through objects pulled from the ground. One part of reading Celtic myth means working out what's old and genuine underneath the later reworking. Patricia Monaghan lays out the problem:

> "Because the Celts did not write down their myths but instead recited them, there is no single source or scripture that defines Celtic mythology. In addition, sculptures and inscriptions date from the Roman invasion and were subject to the reinterpretation of Celtic deities according to the Roman pantheon; thus it is sometimes difficult to clearly distinguish what the Celts originally believed. Finally, many literary sources were transcribed by Christian monks, leaving open the possibility that the written myths themselves may be tainted with post-Celtic material." (1)

This is the problem that underlies the whole field. There's no Celtic Homer, no Hesiod, no holy book set down while any purely Celtic religion was still alive. What we have instead are later versions of spoken texts, made by people who already belonged to a different religious culture.

James MacKillop sums up what happened:

> "Early Irish and Welsh texts were written by Christian scribes, often unmistakably hostile to the 'pagan' traditions that preceded them. Deities of the older religion are reduced to tantalizingly shadowy phantoms ... Or they are historicized into kings, queens, or heroes, ..." (2)

"Historicized" is an important idea here. In Ireland we clearly see how myth was turned into a kind of imagined history: the old gods were written as kings and chiefs, heroes, saints, or fairies, and placed in family trees (3). Gods became great-great-grandfathers. Battles between divine powers became chapters in the story of a nation. So when you read one of these texts, you often can't tell whether you're looking at a war among gods or a saga about long-dead kings. The different periods in the story's evolution have become blurred.

The Irish text *Cath Maige Tuired* (The Second Battle of Mag Tuired) is probably the best place to see the older layer still showing through. It tells of the Túatha Dé Danann, a race of divine beings, arriving in Ireland with four treasures of enormous power:

> "The Túatha Dé Danann were in the northern islands of the world, studying occult lore and sorcery, druidic arts and witchcraft and magical skill, until they surpassed the sages of the pagan arts. They studied occult lore and secret knowledge and diabolic arts in four cities: Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias. From Falias was brought the Stone of Fál which was located in Tara. It used to cry out beneath every king that would take Ireland. From Gorias was brought the spear which Lug had. No battle was ever sustained against it, or against the man who held it in his hand. From Findias was brought the sword of Núadu. No one ever escaped from it once it was drawn from its deadly sheath, and no one could resist it. From Murias was brought the Dagda's cauldron. No company ever went away from it unsatisfied." (4)

Each of these four objects stands for something greater: the right to rule, the power to win every fight, justice, and plenty. And yet the very same text treats the Túatha Dé as a real people: invaders with a backstory and a family tree. The two layers, the divine and the imagined history, overlap each other, and neither one dominates. Turning the symbols of myth into historical legend is exactly how medieval Irish and Welsh writers tended to handle this old material (5).

**Deeper reading:** It's easy to read the historicising as pure loss: the monks flattening gods into kings and burying what they really were. But an early medieval scribe who still believed in the Túatha Dé as real gods might not have wished to fit them into a Christian frame. And on the other hand a scribe who was proud of his native tradition might still have wanted to preserve them in an acceptable Christian form, whilst another sought only to use them to further promote the new religion. The point being, not all Christina scribes had the same relationship to the native tradition. Some texts clearly preserve pre- or at least non-Christian material, whilst others have been heavily Christianised. 

## How can we trust the medieval texts if Christian monks rewrote them?

They earn our trust, quite simply, because they don't stand alone. The same gods and story-shapes keep turning up right across the Celtic world, and in the wider family of Indo-European cultures that share its deep roots. That repetition gives scholars a way to test what's genuinely old. MacKillop makes the case: for all the rewriting, the Irish and Welsh stories

> "contain countless parallels in motif, theme, and character with other Indo-European traditions we usually call 'mythology', not only Greek and Roman, but Norse, Slavic, Iranian, and Indian. They also demonstrate abundant parallels with continental Celtic religion, beginning with the Irish Lug Lámfhota, the Welsh Lleu Llaw Gyffes, and the Gaulish Lugos." (6)

Macleod fills this in with names you can set side by side: the early Irish worshipped a god called Lug, while the Celts on the mainland honoured one called Lugos; the Irish had a god named Ogma, the Gauls a god named Ogmios; the British Celts had a goddess called Brigantia, the Irish a goddess called Bríg (7). The same gods turn up from Ireland to Gaul, wearing slightly different names. That kind of agreement, right across the Celtic world, gives us a real grip on what lies behind the medieval manuscripts.

**Deeper reading:** The Irish monk who reworked Lug into a half-historical chieftain had never seen a Gaulish inscription to Lugos, and probably couldn't have read one if he had. The Welshman writing about Lleu wasn't comparing notes with anyone on the Continent. None of them came together to agree on what they would preserve and make sure it was similar. So the differences between Lug and Lleu are on the surface, where each scribe worked alone; the shared inheritance sits underneath, where they couldn't reach it. When the names line up across hundreds of miles and centuries, it isn't because someone tidied them up so that they matched. It's because something genuinely old is still showing through.

## What actually counts as "Celtic mythology"?

There isn't one fixed thing called Celtic mythology. Instead, there are three different bodies of evidence that don't always fully agree: the medieval manuscripts, the descriptions left by Greek and Roman writers, and the objects archaeologists dig up. The oldest part of what we call "Celtic mythology" is really what comes into focus when you read all three against each other, rather than anything you could point to from a single source.

That third strand, the archaeology, has its own problems. Maxim Fomin points out that the digs, especially at the Hallstatt and La Tène sites (the two oldest periods in Celtic history), back up some of what the stories and poems tell us: that kingship mattered, that ritual mattered, that society was sharply ranked. The finds also show just how much meaning early Celtic art could pack into a single image. But the material is broken and partial, and hard to read dependably (8). A carved stone warrior can hint at ritual, at high status, maybe even at a god, but it can't tell you a single story anyone told about him.

Fomin puts it clearly:

> "But which mythology is Celtic? Is it the one constructed by medieval texts, where the Indo-European beliefs are epitomized in the figures of the gods of Tuatha Dé Danann? Is it the one described by the Classical authors in their observations of the religious practices of the Celts? Or is it the belief system reconstructed from the material remains of the Celtic age, exemplified by the sandstone statue of the warrior priest of Glauberg? The answer is still uncertain, although the ongoing discussion of texts and artefacts, myths and material data promises to yield further insights." (9)

That open question isn't a sign our studies have failed, but a transparent picture of what the evidence is like. People have been trying to sort and make sense of this material since at least the early twentieth century, scholars like Richard Irvine Best and Marie-Henri d'Arbois de Jubainville did the early spadework that others built on for decades (10). What they handed down wasn't a myth-system recovered whole. It was a way of reading the fragments well.

So what we call Celtic mythology is really several traditions laid over one another: stories half-saved in medieval manuscripts, the same god-names echoing across Europe, traces of ritual left in metal and stone, and the deeper patterns that tie all of it back to a shared Indo-European past. Reading it well means keeping these strands in view at the same time, watching where they line up, and being transparent about where they don't.

**Deeper reading:** Notice why no single one of these strands can stand in for Celtic mythology on its own. The carved stone is solid and real, but mute, it hands you an object and no story. The manuscript hands you the story, but no promise it's old rather than something a monk made up. The matching god-names prove that something ancient came through, but on their own they're just a list. Each kind of evidence is strong in exactly the place where the others are weak. That's why scholars read them against each other rather than crowning a favourite: each strand covers the others' blind spots, and only together do they add up to anything you could fairly call Celtic mythology.

## My own thoughts . . .

What is Celtic mythology today and can we really pin it down? This is an attempt to ask the awkward question underneath the usual answers, following the tradition from medieval manuscripts into far stranger territory: why most of it now happens in English, the long shadow of Tolkien, and a sprawling modern world that has grown into the living definition. The lines are blurry, and what Celtic mythology is today may be moving out of our hands entirely.
