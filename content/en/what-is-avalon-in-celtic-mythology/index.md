Avalon is the island far out to the west where the dying King Arthur is carried after his last battle. We first meet the name in a book by Geoffrey of Monmouth called the *Historia Regum Britanniae* (the History of the Kings of Britain), written around 1139. But Geoffrey didn't dream it up. He was drawing on a much older Celtic idea: a blessed island out west, across the sea, where time runs slow, wounds heal, and magical women hold power. The name belongs to a whole family of "apple-island" place-names that turns up right across the Celtic world, from Wales to Ireland to one spot in Roman Burgundy. And the woman who rules there carries the memory of priestesses and otherworld queens from centuries before anyone ever told a story about Arthur.

## 1. What did Geoffrey of Monmouth write about Avalon?

Geoffrey of Monmouth wrote about Avalon in two books, finished decades apart, and it's from him that the familiar picture comes. In the first, his History of the Kings of Britain, he hardly mentions it at all:

> "The illustrious king Arthur too was wounded; he was taken away to the island of Avallon to have his wounds tended and, in the year of Our Lord 542, handed over Britain's crown to his relative Constantinus, son of Cador duke of Cornwall." (1)

The crown passes, the year gets recorded, and Arthur simply vanishes into the west. Geoffrey tells us nothing about the place. He gives no map, no description or word on what it is. The island takes the king, and the story moves past it.

His second book, the *Vita Merlini* (Life of Merlin), is far more open-handed. Here Avalon becomes a real place, with detail you can picture:

> "In another work, *Vita Merlini* (Life of Merlin), Geoffrey gave further details: Avalon was an island of apples, a place where, without cultivation, the land produced fruit and grain. A woman named Morgen ruled there, the wisest and most beautiful of nine sisters. She had the ability to fly and was said to have instructed her sisters in mathematics. She promised Arthur's companions that if the king remained on the island with her, he would recover through her healing arts." (2)

The land that feeds itself, the nine magical women, the promise of healing with no guarantee the king ever returns: none of it is Geoffrey inventing. He's writing down a tradition much older than he is.

**Deeper reading:** Geoffrey gives us the exact year Arthur left, 542, and the name of the cousin who got the crown, all the tidy furniture of history. Then he ships the king off to an island he won't describe at all. The precise date and the placeless island sit right next to each other, and Geoffrey feels no strain between the two. For him, a 'historical' king of Britain and a mythical western otherworld could share one sentence with no trouble at all.

## 2. What does the name Avalon mean?

Avalon means, near enough, "apple island." The name grows from the old Celtic word for apple, which you can still hear in Welsh *afal* and Irish *uball* or *aball*. Both of those come down from one older word, and a medieval Irish scholar, Cormac wrote a famous glossary (a kind of early dictionary) whose note on it is worth a look:

> "Uball ('an apple') quasi aball : Aball autem from a town of Italy whereunto is the name Abellanium : thence they brought the seed of the apples." (3)

Cormac's guess that the word came from an Italian town is wrong, it's the kind of story people told about words before the study of language history existed. But it does show how much the apple mattered to early Irish word-lore. And the apple-island idea reaches well past Ireland. Rachel Bromwich, studying the Welsh *Triads* (a body of old lore sorted into groups of three), points out a nice double meaning hiding in the Welsh name:

> "since Afallach is also a noun meaning 'a place of apples', there is room for doubt as to whether the proper or the common noun is present in the name Ynys Avallach… Chotzen has shown that *abhlach* occurs in Irish as an adjective with reference to the Otherworld island which was the home of the god Manannán mac Lir, and which had come to be variously located on the Scottish island of Arran, and on the Isle of Man." (4)

So the Welsh name *Ynys Afallach* can be read two ways at once: "island of apple trees," or "island of Afallach," an old ancestor-figure. The Irish *Emain Ablach*, home of the sea-god Manannán mac Lir, sits in exactly the same spot. James MacKillop spells out the match: "A name for the Avalon of Arthurian tradition in certain medieval narratives, attributing Welsh origin, is *Insula Pomorum*, 'The Isle of Apples'. One gloss of the name for the magical Irish island Emain Ablach is 'Emain of the Apples'." (5) So Geoffrey's *insula pomorum*, his "isle of apples," was not a fresh coinage at all: the phrase was already there in the tradition, and all he did was put it into Latin.

## 3. Is Avalon the same as the Celtic Otherworld?

Yes, in effect. Avalon is one version of the Celtic Otherworld, the realm of the gods, immortals and sometimes the dead that the old stories set apart from ours, usually out west across the sea. It shares its main features with a whole cluster of these blessed islands. Patricia Monaghan sets out what such a place looks like:

> "Avalon was a beautiful place of mild weather and eternal spring, where flowers and fruit were found simultaneously on the trees and where no storms ever raged. A queen ruled there, sometimes named as Argante; she and her maiden attendants were great healers… To the Celts, Avalon was clearly one of those western isles of the Otherworld where the dead and the divine mingle." (6)

Endless spring, blossom and fruit on the tree at the same time, healing women, a home in the west: all of these turns up again in the Welsh *Annwn* and the Irish *Tír na nÓg* and *Mag Mell*, all names for that same otherworld. Sharon Paice MacLeod pushes the idea back even further, and wonders whether there was once a real island behind it:

> "There may have been an island known in ancient times as Aballus, which was later reflected in medieval British legends as Avallach or Avalon, and as Emain Ablach in Ireland. We can see numerous parallels between medieval references to Arthur's journeys to Annwfn or Avalon, and historical (or pseudo-historical) references to sacred islands which served as sanctuaries to communities of holy people engaged in devotional practices and sacrificial rites." (7)

Here too, Geoffrey was joining a tradition already long in motion, not starting one.

## 4. Who are the nine women of Avalon?

The nine women who rule Avalon, led by Morgen, aren't unique either. Groups of nine magical women show up again and again in Celtic and even Roman sources, long before Geoffrey wrote. Angelika Heike Rüdiger lines several of them up side by side:

> "Avallon, Annwn and Caer Loyw are all inhabited by a triple triad of women with supernatural powers. Haycock and Loomis note the parallels between Morgen and her sisters, who dwell on Geoffrey's *Insula Pomorum*, and the nine maidens guarding the cauldron of Annwn in '*Preiddeu Annwn*'… We also find nine women with magical powers in a classical source, Pomponius Mela's *De Chorographia* speaking of the Gallicenas of Sena." (8)

Pomponius Mela, a Roman writer, set this down around AD 43, a good 500 years before anyone was telling Arthur stories. On the island of Sena, off the coast of Armorica (roughly today's Brittany), he described nine virgin priestesses who could heal the sick and foretell the future. Centuries later the Welsh poem *Preiddeu Annwn* (The Spoils of Annwn), written down by the tenth century at the latest, puts nine maidens around the cauldron of the Otherworld. Geoffrey's nine sisters on Avalon make a third corner of that triangle, and easily the latest. Sharon Paice MacLeod notes that Geoffrey's Morgen is "noted for her wisdom and learning, her healing powers, and the ability to shapeshift" (9), a set of gifts that looks far more like the old pre-Christian holy woman than like anything from the later romances.

**Deeper reading:** A Roman writer counts nine priestesses on an island in the first century. A Welsh poet sets nine maidens round a cauldron a thousand years later. Geoffrey gives Morgen eight sisters, making nine again. The stories change nearly everything else: the names, the setting, even the language they're told in. But the count of the women persists. Whatever these nine were to the people who first imagined them, they mattered enough that the shape outlasted almost everything around it.

## 5. How does King Arthur get to Avalon?

Arthur is carried across the water to Avalon by boat, borne away by mysterious women after his last battle. That sea-crossing to an island in the west is one of the oldest story-shapes the Celts had. The Irish kept a whole class of tales about it, the *imrama* (voyage stories), about sailing out to magical islands. One old study called them:

> "very ancient; and though indefinite in their results, and burdened with much matter of a poetic or romantic character, there can be no rational doubt that they are founded on fact." (10)

Arthur's last voyage belongs with these. Fañch Bihan-Gallic notes:

> "the idea of crossing water toward the afterlife is found quite famously in the Matter of Britain, which finds its origin both in Welsh and Breton legends… no source ever noted Arthur's return from Avalon, which makes it de facto his otherworldly resting place. The *Brut y Brenhinedd* even unambiguously states that Arthur is buried on Avalon, which is an original statement from this Welsh translation of Geoffrey's *Historia*." (11)

This is what makes the "once and future king" idea work: nobody ever quite says whether he's dead. Patricia Monaghan puts it like this:

> "Arthur was mortally wounded. He did not die, however; casting Excalibur into a lake, he was borne across its waters to Avalon by mysterious women, thus becoming the 'once and future king'." (12)

The island never settles whether Arthur lives or dies, it keeps the question hanging, and that suspense is the whole point.

## 6. Is Avalon the same place as Glastonbury?

Glastonbury, a town in Somerset, has been claimed as the real Avalon since the twelfth century, but most scholars think the link was invented. In 1191 the monks there said they had dug up the bodies of Arthur and Guinevere, along with a lead cross naming the place *Avalonia*. The timing was convenient. The abbey had just burned down and badly needed money, and a grave belonging to Arthur would pull in pilgrims by the cartload. John Koch judges the words on the cross to be written in a form no sixth-century hand could have used, and treats the whole thing as a medieval fake. Helen Fulton's survey backs him up. She notes that as far back as 1777 Thomas Warton had already "distinguished between the historico-chronicle tradition, which focused on Christian Glastonbury as the site of Arthur's burial, and the romance tradition, which favored the notion of the grave as an 'otherworldly' island." (13)

Others have gone looking for the name somewhere else altogether. As Monaghan notes, some "have derived the word Avalon, used of the mysterious and powerful place of Arthurian legend, from the phrase Emain Ablach ('island of apples'), used to describe the Otherworld." (14) Naming Glastonbury as Avalon tames something the Celtic tradition had worked hard to keep mysterious and out of reach.

**Deeper reading:** In the last two sections you can watch two instincts pull against each other. The older tradition needs Avalon to stay out of reach and Arthur's death to stay unsettled, and that unsettledness is the very thing that lets him be the king who might come back. The Glastonbury monks needed the opposite. A living king who's merely gone away brings no pilgrims. A marked grave you can stand over does. So they took a story built to keep its ending open and gave it a body, a cross, and a plot of consecrated ground. The wonder and the money were pulling in opposite directions.

## 7. What is the Welsh tradition behind Avalon?

Long before Arthur, Welsh tradition already had an Avalon of a sort, tied not to a king but to an ancestor. His name was Afallach, son of Beli Mawr, and he turns up in old Welsh family-trees from at least the tenth century. Rachel Bromwich finds the same double meaning here as in the place-name. Geoffrey, she says:

> "borrowed the name Aballac either from Harl. Gen. I [a genealogy manuscript] or from another genealogy, and he included him among the sons of Ebraucus… But since Afallach is also a noun meaning 'a place of apples', there is room for doubt as to whether the proper or the common noun is present in the name Ynys Avallach." (15)

Afallach had a daughter, Modron, a mother-goddess figure whose name comes from the older Gaulish *Matrona*, "the great mother." Scholars including Loomis have matched her with Morgain la Fée from the Arthurian romances, though as words the two names aren't actually related. The same otherworld thinking that *Ynys Afallach* belongs to shows up in the Mabinogi, the great collection of medieval Welsh tales. In the Third Branch, a kingdom that a spell had emptied suddenly comes back:

> "'Look around you at the land,' he said, 'and you will see all the houses and dwelling-places as they were at their best.' Then Manawydan got up and looked around. And when he looked, he could see all the land inhabited and complete with all its herds and its houses." (16)

That's the same logic underneath both *Ynys Afallach* and *Annwn*, a kingdom held in a kind of pause, waiting to be entered or set right again. Jaan Puhvel points to the direct link. In the *Mabinogi*, Pwyll "also mysteriously acquires condominium in the Otherworld (Annwn; cf. the Arthurian Avalon) before meeting and marrying the Lady on the White Horse, Rhiannon." (17)

**Deeper reading:** Afallach does two things at once. It names a place, the isle of apples, and it names a person, an ancestor in the old family-trees. To us those are separate things, a location and a man. To the Welsh who kept these names, they weren't so easily pried apart. The island could be the ancestor and the ancestor could be the island depending on which story needed telling.

Avalon, then, is what you get when an old, shared Celtic idea, a blessed island in the west, full of magical women, reachable only by sea, gets fastened onto one man and one story. Geoffrey gave it a Latin name, but the idea had been drifting through Welsh and Irish for centuries: in apple place-names from Cumbria all the way to Burgundy, in the nine maidens warming their cauldron in the Otherworld, in the priestesses of Sena. Geoffrey didn't invent Avalon but he fixed it, the way water sets into ice, and ever since it's been harder to see the older thing clearly through it.
