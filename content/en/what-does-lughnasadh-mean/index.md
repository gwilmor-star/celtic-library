Lughnasadh is a Celtic harvest festival that reportedly takes place on August 1st, etymologically derived from the combination of Lugh (the Irish god of crafts and skills) and násad (assembly), meaning "the assembly of Lugh".

## Key Facts

- The name derives from Old Irish *Lugnasad*, combining *Lug* (the god Lugh) and *násad* (assembly).
- Cormac's Glossary defines it as "a commemorating game or fair" celebrated by Lugh mac Ethne "in the beginning of autumn".
- The festival marks the beginning of the harvest season and falls in August.
- Modern Irish preserves the name as *Lúnasa*, which is also the word for August.

## Q&A Sections

### What does the etymology of Lughnasadh reveal about its meaning?

The etymology directly connects the festival to the god Lugh and the concept of assembly. Cormac's Glossary explains that it is "a commemorating game or fair, thereto is the name nasad i.e. a festival or game of Lugh mac Ethne". The Old Irish form *Lugnasad* combines *Lug* (the god Lugh) with *násad* (assembly), though some scholars suggest an alternative derivation from *nás* (death) rather than *násad*. This etymology establishes the festival as fundamentally associated with Lugh's commemorative gathering.

### How is Lughnasadh connected to the harvest season?

Lughnasadh marks "the end of summer and the beginning of the harvest" and falls in August. Folklorist Máire MacNeill documented that the festival's main theme involves "a struggle for the harvest between two gods" where Lugh must seize grain from another deity for mankind. The customs included "the cutting of the first corn" and ritual performances representing Lugh. This connection to harvest activities reflects the festival's agricultural significance in Celtic society.

### What role does the god Lugh play in the festival?

Lugh appears as the central divine figure who established and is honored by the festival. According to the sources, Lughnasadh was "celebrated by him in the beginning of autumn". In later folklore traditions, Lugh becomes the hero who "must seize" the harvest "for mankind" in ritual combat with opposing forces. The god is described as the deity "of crafts and skills", and the festival serves as his commemorative assembly or *násad*.

### When does Lughnasadh occur in the Celtic calendar?

The festival "falls in August" and traditionally occurs on August 1st, though sources do not confirm the specific date (sources limited). This timing places it at one of the cross-quarter days in the Celtic year, marking the midpoint between the summer solstice and autumn equinox. The festival represents one of the seasonal celebrations in the Celtic calendar, positioned as the beginning of the harvest season when "the first of the harvest could be gathered".

## In-Depth Overview

### Linguistic Origins and Etymology

The name Lughnasadh preserves ancient Celtic religious terminology that reveals the festival's divine associations. Cormac's Glossary provides the explicit definition: "LUGNASAD .i.e. a commemorating game or fair, thereto is the name nasad i.e. a festival or game of Lugh mac Ethne or Ethlenn, which was celebrated by him in the beginning of autumn". This definition establishes both the festival's connection to Lugh and its autumnal timing.

The linguistic evolution shows continuity from Old Irish *Lugnasad* through various medieval spellings to Modern Irish *Lúnasa*, which serves as both the festival name and the word for August. The compound structure combining the divine name with *násad* (assembly) follows established patterns in Celtic religious terminology, where festivals were understood as gatherings initiated or presided over by particular deities.

### Mythological Framework

The festival's mythological significance extends beyond simple harvest celebration to encompass themes of divine conflict and seasonal transition. Later folklore traditions, as documented by Máire MacNeill, preserve narratives where "the main theme is a struggle for the harvest between two gods". In these stories, Lugh confronts an opposing deity, often called Crom Dubh, who "guards the grain as his treasure" while Lugh "must seize it for mankind".

These mythological patterns connect Lughnasadh to broader Celtic concepts of seasonal kingship and divine intervention in agricultural cycles. The Acallam na Senórach references the concept of *Lugnasad* in contexts that suggest seasonal rather than solely festival meanings, noting that it "seems to have the meaning here of a season rather than a specific harvest festival". This broader seasonal association reinforces the festival's role in marking major transitions in the Celtic year.

### Archaeological and Historical Context

The festival's antiquity is suggested by references in early Irish literature and the persistence of associated place-names across Celtic regions. The *Táin* and related texts contain references to Lugaid and related figures, while the widespread distribution of *Lugdunum* place-names across continental Europe indicates the pan-Celtic significance of Lugh worship. Cities including "Lyons, Laon, and Leiden all represent an earlier Lugudunum" and Carlisle preserves *Luguvalium*.

Archaeological evidence for specific Lughnasadh practices remains limited, but the festival's integration into the broader Celtic calendar system is documented through medieval sources. The *Battle of Mag Tuired* presents Lugh as "the omnicompetent hero" who establishes dominance across "the domains of kingship, martial prowess, and agriculture", providing mythological precedent for harvest-time celebrations honoring his comprehensive abilities.

### Ritual and Traditional Practices

Medieval and later sources document specific customs associated with Lughnasadh celebrations. The festival involved "the cutting of the first corn; a 'ritual dance-play' and other plays representing Lugh". These dramatic elements suggest that the festival included theatrical reenactments of mythological narratives, particularly those involving Lugh's conflicts with opposing forces.

The sources describe the festival as "a commemorating game or fair" indicating that Lughnasadh functioned as both religious observance and social gathering. The *násad* element specifically denotes assembly or festival activities, suggesting communal participation rather than purely private ritual observance.

## Primary Sources

***Cormac's Glossary (Sanas Cormaic)*** *c. 900 AD*
*transl. in: O'Donovan & Stokes ed. (1868)*
> LUGNASAD .i.e. a commemorating game or fair, thereto is the name nasad i.e. a festival or game of Lugh mac Ethne or Ethlenn, which was celebrated by him in the beginning of autumn.

***Ancient Irish Tales*** *c. 9th century (manuscript tradition)*
*in: Cross & Slover eds. (1936)*
> Lugnasad is the nasad of Lug: nasad is an assembly or festival in commemoration or memorial of a death. Now the Fir Bolg were all slaughtered in that battle, as we have said, save a few; and those of them who survived fled before the Tuatha De into the outermost isles and islets of the sea, so that they dwelt in them after that.

***Acallam na Senórach*** *c. 1200 AD*
*transl. in: Dooley & Roe (1999)*
> Trogan, or Lugnasad: Lugnasad seems to have the meaning here of a season rather than a specific harvest festival. MacNeill, Festival of Lughnasa, lists no tradition of a Lugnasad festival on Arran.

***Cath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired)*** *c. 9th century*
*transl. in: Stokes (1891)*
> 'There is in sooth. Tell your brehon that for Bres the kine of Erin shall always be under the yoke if he be spared.' 'That does not save thee either', says Lugh. 'What then will save me?' says Bres. 'This indeed,' says Lugh. 'When shall the kine of Erin be milked? when shall they be calved? when shall they be under the yoke? if thou tellest us these things, thou shalt be spared for the sake of that boon.'

***Coire Sois: The Cauldron of Knowledge*** *medieval manuscript sources*
*in: Ó Cathasaigh (2014)*
> the one which continued to be celebrated in the harvest-festival of Lughnasadh, the survival of which into our own time has been shown and documented by Máire MacNeill. Writing of CMT, MacNeill gave it as her view that Núadu, Balar, and Bres are three aspects of Lug's opponent, but I would prefer to see Lug's successive victories over this trio as a sequence in which he establishes his pre-eminence in the domains of kingship, martial prowess, and agriculture.

***Trioedd Ynys Prydein*** *c. 13th century*
*in: Bromwich ed. (2006)*
> The epithet llawgyffes 'skilful hand' bestowed on Lleu in the naming-incident in Math is curiously paralleled in meaning by the Ir. samildánach which is given to Lugh in the Ir. tale Cath Maige Tuired: the Battle of Mag Tuired. Samildánach means 'skilled in many arts together', and both this and the W. llawgyffes express the same conception of the hero's skill and dexterity.

***The Táin (Táin Bó Cúailnge)*** *c. 7th–8th century*
*transl. in: Kinsella (1969/2002)*
> Forgall Monach heard of this and came to Temair. He told Lugaid he had an unmarried girl at home who was the finest in Ireland for shapeliness and purity and tidiness. Lugaid was glad to hear it, and Forgall promised the girl to him, and the Breg landowners' twelve daughters to Lugaid's twelve princes as well.
