Lughnasadh (also spelled Lughnasa or Lugnasad) is an ancient Celtic harvest festival meaning "the assembly or commemorative festival of Lugh," derived from the Irish god Lugh and the word "nasad," which signifies an assembly or festival in commemoration of a death.

## Key Facts

- Lughnasadh literally means "nasad of Lug," where nasad refers to "an assembly or festival in commemoration or memorial of a death".
- Cormac's Glossary defines it as "a commemorating game or fair" celebrating "a festival or game of Lugh mac Ethne or Ethlenn, which was celebrated by him in the beginning of autumn".
- The festival was associated with the Irish god Lugh (also called Lug Lamfada "Long-Arm"), a chief deity of the Tuatha Dé Danann skilled in many arts.
- In some contexts, Lughnasadh appears to denote a season rather than a specific harvest festival.

## Q&A Sections

### What is the literal meaning of the word Lughnasadh?

The name Lughnasadh combines two Irish elements: "Lug" (referring to the god Lugh) and "nasad." According to the medieval Irish text Cath Maige Tuired, "Lugnasad is the nasad of Lug: nasad is an assembly or festival in commemoration or memorial of a death". Cormac's Glossary similarly defines it as "a commemorating game or fair". The commemorative aspect suggests the festival honored someone who had died, though the exact identity varies in different sources.

### Who was Lugh in Celtic mythology?

Lugh (spelled Lug in older Irish sources) was one of the most prominent deities of the Tuatha Dé Danann, the supernatural race that ruled Ireland before the arrival of the Milesians. He was known as "Lug Lamfada" meaning "Lug Long-Arm". In the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, he is called "Samildánach," meaning "skilled in many arts". Lugh's epithet as master of all crafts made him a central figure in Celtic religion, with cities across western Europe named after him, including Lyon, Laon, and Leiden.

### When was Lughnasadh celebrated?

According to Cormac's Glossary, Lughnasadh was "celebrated by him in the beginning of autumn". This places the festival at the start of the harvest season, marking the transition from summer to autumn in the Celtic calendar. In some medieval Irish sources, the term appears to refer to a seasonal period rather than a single day, suggesting it may have encompassed an extended time of celebration during the early harvest.

### What was the purpose of Lughnasadh?

The festival served as both a harvest celebration and a commemorative assembly. The "nasad" element indicates it was "an assembly or festival in commemoration or memorial of a death". Cormac's Glossary describes it as "a commemorating game or fair", suggesting it combined religious observance with practical activities like games, trading, and community gatherings. The timing at the beginning of autumn made it a crucial agricultural festival marking the start of the harvest season.

## In-Depth Overview

### Etymology and Linguistic Analysis

The word Lughnasadh represents one of the clearest examples of theophoric naming in the Celtic calendar, directly linking a seasonal festival to a specific deity. The compound consists of "Lug," the genitive form of the god's name, and "nasad," a term that medieval Irish sources consistently translate as referring to commemorative assemblies. The Second Battle of Mag Tuired provides the most explicit definition: "Lugnasad is the nasad of Lug: nasad is an assembly or festival in commemoration or memorial of a death".

Cormac's Glossary, compiled around 900 AD, offers additional detail, describing Lughnasadh as "a commemorating game or fair, thereto is the name nasad i.e. a festival or game of Lugh mac Ethne or Ethlenn, which was celebrated by him in the beginning of autumn". This definition emphasizes both the commemorative nature and the autumnal timing of the celebration.

### The God Lugh in Celtic Tradition

Lugh occupies a central position in the Irish pantheon as a deity associated with skill, craftsmanship, and sovereignty. In the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, he is called "Samildánach," meaning "skilled in many arts", and appears as "Lug Lamfada" or "Lug Long-Arm" in various sources. His importance extended far beyond Ireland, as evidenced by the numerous European cities bearing his name, including Lyon (Lugdunum), Laon, and Leiden, all representing earlier forms of Lugudunum.

The Triads of the Island of Britain note that "Caesar (De Bell. Gall. VI, 17) states that the Gauls worship Mercury beyond all other gods, hunt omnium inventorem artium ferunt. Combined with the widespread Gaulish dedications of cities to Lugus, Caesar's account suggests that by Mercury he meant Lugus, the most widely worshipped of all Celtic deities". This widespread veneration suggests that festivals associated with Lugh, including Lughnasadh, may have had parallels across the Celtic world.

### Seasonal and Agricultural Significance

The placement of Lughnasadh at "the beginning of autumn" marks it as a crucial transitional festival in the Celtic year. Unlike modern calendar systems, Celtic seasonal divisions often reflected agricultural realities rather than astronomical events. The early autumn timing would have coincided with the first harvests, making Lughnasadh both a celebration of agricultural abundance and a preparation for the darker months ahead.

Some sources suggest the term could refer to an extended seasonal period rather than a single day. The Acallam na Senórach commentary notes that "Lugnasad seems to have the meaning here of a season rather than a specific harvest festival", indicating the flexible nature of the term in medieval Irish literature.

### Commemorative Aspects

The consistent emphasis on commemoration in the etymological sources raises questions about whom or what was being remembered. The explicit definition of "nasad" as "an assembly or festival in commemoration or memorial of a death" suggests that Lughnasadh originally honored a specific deceased figure, though sources differ on the identity. The festival thus combined practical harvest activities with ritual remembrance, creating a complex seasonal observance that addressed both community needs and religious obligations.
