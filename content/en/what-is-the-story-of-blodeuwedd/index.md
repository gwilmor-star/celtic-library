Blodeuwedd is a figure from Welsh mythology who was created from flowers by the magicians Math and Gwydion to be the wife of Lleu Llaw Gyffes. She betrayed her husband by having an affair with Gronw Pebr and plotting Lleu's death, but was ultimately transformed into an owl as punishment.

## Key Facts

- Blodeuwedd was fashioned from the flowers of oak, broom, and meadowsweet by Math and Gwydion as a wife for Lleu Llaw Gyffes.
- She had an affair with Gronw Pebr and conspired with him to kill her husband.
- When Gronw attempted to kill Lleu, Lleu escaped by transforming into an eagle.
- Gwydion caught Blodeuwedd and transformed her into an owl, cursing her to be feared by all other birds.
- Her story is preserved in the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi, one of the most important collections of Welsh medieval tales.

## Q&A Sections

### Why was Blodeuwedd created?

Blodeuwedd was created because Lleu Llaw Gyffes was under a curse that prevented him from having a wife of any race living on earth. His mother Arianrhod had placed this restriction on him as one of three curses. To circumvent this magical prohibition, Math and Gwydion used their powers to fashion a woman from flowers, creating someone who was neither fully human nor of any existing race.

### How did Blodeuwedd betray Lleu?

Blodeuwedd began an affair with Gronw Pebr and conspired with him to kill her husband. She used deception to learn the specific conditions under which Lleu could be killed, as he had special protections. The text suggests parallels with the biblical story of Samson and Delilah, where the wife extracts deadly secrets from her husband. When Gronw attempted the killing, Lleu escaped by transforming into an eagle.

### What was Blodeuwedd's punishment?

When Gwydion caught up with Blodeuwedd, he declared he would not kill her but would do something worse. He transformed her into an owl, changing her name from Blodeuedd ("flowers") to Blodeuwedd ("flower-face") to reflect her new form. The curse included that she would never dare show her face in daylight for fear of all birds, and that all birds would be hostile toward her and strike her wherever they found her.

### What happened to Gronw Pebr?

After Lleu was restored to human form by Gwydion, he demanded recompense from Gronw. Gronw offered compensation, but Lleu insisted on returning the blow that had been struck against him. Gronw pleaded to be allowed to hide behind a stone when Lleu attempted to kill him, and Lleu agreed to this condition. Lleu then reportedly killed Gronw with his spear, though the sources do not confirm whether it pierced through the stone (sources limited).

### What is the significance of the flowers used to create Blodeuwedd?

The specific flowers chosen—oak, broom, and meadowsweet—were significant in Celtic tradition. These plants may have had symbolic meanings related to the seasonal cycle and fertility. The oak was particularly sacred in Celtic culture, while broom and meadowsweet were associated with purification and sweetness. Her creation from flowers represents the artificial nature of her being and foreshadows her ultimate fate, as she was neither fully human nor bound by human loyalties (sources limited).

## In-Depth Overview

### The Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi

Blodeuwedd's story forms a central part of the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi, known as "Math fab Mathonwy". This branch deals with themes of magical creation, betrayal, and transformation that are characteristic of Welsh medieval literature. The tale demonstrates the consequences of attempting to circumvent natural order through magic, as Math and Gwydion's creation ultimately brings destruction upon the family.

### The Creation Myth

The creation of Blodeuwedd represents one of the most striking examples of magical artifice in Welsh literature. Math and Gwydion "took the flowers of the oak, and the flowers of the broom, and the flowers of the meadowsweet, and from these they conjured up the fairest and most beautiful maiden that anyone had ever seen". This creation was necessitated by Arianrhod's curse that prevented Lleu from having a wife of any earthly race. The flower-woman represents an attempt to create life without the participation of the feminine divine, highlighting themes of masculine control over creation and its ultimate failure.

### The Betrayal and Its Consequences

Blodeuwedd's betrayal of Lleu follows a pattern common in Celtic literature where supernatural beings or artificially created characters often prove unreliable or dangerous to their creators. Her affair with Gronw Pebr and the conspiracy to kill Lleu demonstrates the impossibility of creating loyalty through magical means. The text notes parallels with the biblical story of Samson and Delilah, where the wife extracts deadly secrets from her supernaturally protected husband.

### Transformation and Punishment

The transformation of Blodeuwedd into an owl represents both punishment and a form of truth-telling, as her new form reflects her nocturnal and predatory nature. Gwydion's curse that "you will never dare show your face in daylight for fear of all the birds" creates a lasting enmity between owls and other birds that explains a natural phenomenon through mythological narrative. The name change from Blodeuedd ("flowers") to Blodeuwedd ("flower-face") marks her permanent transformation from creation to creature.

### Literary and Cultural Context

The story preserves strong associations with specific geographical locations in Wales, "perhaps more so than any other tale in the corpus". Places mentioned include Bryn Cyfergyr ("Hill of the Blow") and Nantlleu ("Valley of Lleu"), suggesting the tale was used to explain local place names and topographical features. The narrative also appears in the Welsh Triads, where Blodeuwedd's retinue is listed as one of the "Three Disloyal Retinues".

### Variant Traditions

Alternative versions of the Blodeuwedd story exist in Welsh literature. A poem attributed to Dafydd ap Gwilym refers to a variant where Blodeuwedd's lover is named "Goronwy fab Pefr Goronhir, Arglwydd Penllyn" rather than the Gronw Pebr of the Mabinogi. These variations suggest the story had a living tradition that continued to evolve in medieval Wales, with different regions or storytellers preserving slightly different versions of the core narrative.
