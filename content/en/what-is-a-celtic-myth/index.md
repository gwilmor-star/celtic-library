Celtic myth refers to the traditional stories and oral narratives of the Celtic peoples across Europe, encompassing supernatural beings, heroic adventures, and otherworldly encounters that were transmitted through generations before being recorded in medieval manuscripts.

## Key Facts

- Celtic mythology originates from the polytheistic religious traditions of Iron Age Celtic peoples across Europe.
- Continental Celtic myths largely disappeared following Roman conquest and Christianisation, surviving mainly through Greco-Roman sources and archaeology.
- Most surviving Celtic mythology comes from Irish and Welsh vernacular sources preserved in medieval manuscripts.
- Celtic myths feature supernatural beings including gods, spirits, and otherworldly creatures like the Tuatha Dé Danann.
- These narratives encompass creation stories, heroic tales, and otherworld adventures that reflect Celtic cultural values and cosmology.

## Q&A Sections

### What are the main characteristics of Celtic mythology?
Celtic mythology consists of traditional narratives featuring supernatural beings, gods, and heroes that reflect the polytheistic religious beliefs of ancient Celtic peoples. These myths typically involve otherworldly encounters, magical transformations, and interactions between mortal heroes and divine figures. The stories often explore themes of life, death, rebirth, and the liminal boundaries between the mortal world and supernatural realms.

### How were Celtic myths preserved and transmitted?
Celtic myths were originally transmitted through oral tradition, passed down from generation to generation by storytellers and bards. Much of early Celtic mythology was orally transmitted and only later recorded in medieval texts, often influenced by Christianisation. The oral tradition aimed at preserving and transmitting stories of the Celtic peoples without writing, meaning the first written texts about ancient Celts were often composed by foreigners.

### What happened to Continental Celtic mythology?
The mythologies of continental Celtic peoples, such as the Gauls and Celtiberians, did not survive their conquest by the Roman Empire, the loss of their Celtic languages, and subsequent conversion to Christianity. Only remnants of continental Celtic myths are found in Greco-Roman sources and archaeological evidence. Archaeological discoveries, particularly ancient artifacts like cauldrons and iconographic studies, provide critical insight into prehistoric Celtic beliefs that were otherwise lost.

### What types of stories appear in Celtic mythology?
Celtic myths encompass creation stories, heroic tales, and otherworld narratives that explore supernatural themes. These traditional narratives feature gods, heroes, and supernatural beings, reflecting the cultural values, beliefs, and historical experiences of Celtic societies. The myths often involve the portrayal of the Otherworld and supernatural motifs, revealing insights into Celtic cosmology and beliefs about life, death, and the metaphysical realm.

### Which Celtic mythologies survived best?
Most surviving Celtic mythology belongs to the Irish and Welsh traditions, preserved in vernacular sources from the Middle Ages. Irish mythology features prominently the Tuatha Dé Danann, medieval reflections of pre-Christian Irish deities. Welsh mythology is preserved in texts like the Mabinogi, which contains elements of Celtic folklore and mythology existing as oral literary tradition.

## In-Depth Overview

### Geographic Origins and Distribution
Celtic mythology originated from the diverse Celtic peoples who inhabited large areas of Europe during the Iron Age. These Celtic-speaking communities extended across Atlantic Europe, with evidence drawn from living Celtic languages, older textual sources, and archaeological research. The Celtic peoples included continental groups such as the Gauls and Celtiberians, as well as the insular Celts of Ireland, Scotland, Wales, and Brittany.

Statistical and archaeological methods reveal non-random distribution patterns of mythological motifs connected with Celtic and broader Eurasian traditions. This research helps reconstruct historical processes of myth transmission and evolution, supporting the identification of core myth motifs potentially traceable in archaeological sources like Celtic iconography.

### Religious and Cultural Context
Celtic peoples followed a polytheistic religion with many gods and goddesses, similar to other Iron Age Europeans. The mythological narratives served multiple functions within Celtic society, picturing the wishes, fears, and values of medieval Irish society while representing the Celtic three-fold cosmology of birth, death, and rebirth.

Celtic supernatural folklore includes various religious figures embodying the veneration of wild nature. The mythological system composed discrete parts and component elements capable of symbolic representation, where the arrangement of symbols with respect to one another held major importance.

### Manuscript Tradition and Sources
The preservation of Celtic myths depended heavily on medieval scribes who recorded oral traditions in manuscript form. Irish and Welsh vernacular sources from the Middle Ages feature various human mythological figures who have been thought by many scholars as being based upon earlier gods. However, historian Ronald Hutton cautioned against automatically characterising all Irish and Welsh mythological figures as euhemerised deities.

Much of the surviving material demonstrates remarkable instances of long-term oral transmission. Scottish Gaelic tradition bearers like Duncan MacDonald could tell certain tales with ties to older literary versions in manuscripts in virtually identical fashion from recitation to recitation.

### Mythological Themes and Motifs
Celtic myths explore various mythological concepts concerning the domestication of space and the taming of wild nature. These narratives often feature the hunt of the deer as the epitome of this process, including its subject (the hunter) and the object (the prey). The stories typically involve themes of liminality, spectral presence, and supernatural dread.

The Otherworld plays a central role in Celtic mythology, with narratives exploring the dualities of the Celtic worldview, including the liminal nature of the Otherworld, the role of magic, and the fluidity of identity in mythic narratives. Celtic mythological symbols and artifacts function as encoded cosmological maps and narratives, with treasures like the Thirteen Treasures and Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann representing zodiacal and seasonal symbolism.
