"Culhwch and Olwen" is a medieval Welsh tale preserved in the Mabinogion that tells the story of Prince Culhwch's quest to win the hand of Olwen, daughter of the giant Ysbaddaden, with the help of his cousin Arthur and his warriors.

## Key Facts
- Culhwch was born in a pig-run (cul-hwch means "pig-sty") and is Arthur's cousin.
- The giant Ysbaddaden sets forty impossible tasks that Culhwch must complete to marry his daughter Olwen.
- Arthur and six of his finest warriors, including Cai and Bedwyr, assist Culhwch in his quest.
- The most prominent task involves hunting the magical boar Twrch Trwyth to obtain a comb and scissors from between its ears.
- After the tasks are completed, Ysbaddaden is killed and Culhwch marries Olwen.

## Q&A Sections
### What causes Culhwch to fall in love with Olwen?
Culhwch's stepmother places a curse on him that "he will marry no one save Olwen, the daughter of Ysbaddaden Chief Giant". At the mere mention of Olwen's name, Culhwch falls in love with her, despite never having seen her. This curse serves as the catalyst for the entire adventure, forcing Culhwch to seek Arthur's help in winning this seemingly impossible match.

### How does Arthur help Culhwch in his quest?
Arthur provides substantial assistance to his cousin Culhwch. He enlists six of his finest warriors as Culhwch's helpers, including Cai, Bedwyr, and Gwalchmei. Arthur also provides a host of other skilled warriors with various abilities. Arthur himself participates directly in the most challenging task, the hunting of Twrch Trwyth, leading the assault against the magical boar.

### What is the significance of the Twrch Trwyth hunt?
The hunt for Twrch Trwyth represents the most elaborate and dangerous of Ysbaddaden's tasks. This supernatural boar carries a comb and scissors between its ears that are required for Ysbaddaden to groom himself for Olwen's wedding. The hunt spans from Ireland across South Wales to Cornwall, involving numerous warriors and resulting in many deaths. The boar hunt has ancient origins, with references appearing in the ninth-century History of the Britons.

### How does Olwen appear when Culhwch first meets her?
When Olwen arrives to meet Culhwch, she is described in highly stylized terms: "with a robe of flame-red silk about her, and a torque of red gold about the maiden's neck, with precious pearls and red jewels. Yellower was her hair than the flowers of the broom. Whiter" was her skin than other comparisons. She comes every Saturday to wash her hair, leaving her rings in the washing bowl.

### What happens to Ysbaddaden at the end of the tale?
After the tasks are completed, Ysbaddaden meets his doom. Gorau son of Custennin "grabbed him by the hair and dragged him to the mound and cut off his head and stuck it on the bailey post". Gorau then takes possession of Ysbaddaden's fort and territory, while "that night Culhwch slept with Olwen. And she was his only wife as long as he lived".

## In-Depth Overview
### Origins and Manuscript Tradition
"Culhwch and Olwen" appears in the medieval Welsh manuscript collection known as the Mabinogion, though fragments occur in manuscripts earlier than the fourteenth-century Red and White Books by about a hundred years. The tale was likely redacted in its present form around 1100 or soon after. Scholars consider it "the oldest Arthurian tale", predating Geoffrey of Monmouth's influential Historia Regum Britanniae by several decades.

### The Hero's Background
The story begins with Cilydd son of Celyddon Wledig seeking a wife as well-born as himself, finding Goleuddydd daughter of Anlawdd Wledig. After conception, Goleuddydd goes mad and avoids dwellings until giving birth in a pig-run while a swineherd tends his herd. The child receives the name Culhwch "because he was found in a pig-run," though the name etymologically combines "cul" (slender) and "hwch" (pig). Culhwch is "of noble descent" and "a cousin to Arthur".

### The Curse and Quest's Beginning
After Goleuddydd's death, Cilydd remarries, and his new wife becomes jealous when Culhwch shows no interest in her daughter from a previous marriage. She curses Culhwch to marry only Olwen, daughter of Ysbaddaden Chief Giant. Upon hearing Olwen's name, Culhwch immediately falls in love and sets off for Arthur's court to seek help.

### Arthur's Court and the List of Warriors
At Arthur's court, Culhwch encounters "a host of men and women whose names are presented in a florid list––some 260 names in all––drawn from a variety of sources, reflecting both historical and legendary characters". This extensive catalogue includes figures ranging from Celtic mythological characters to contemporary medieval personalities, such as "Gwilenhen brenhin freinc" (William king of France), an allusion to William the Conqueror. The list serves both as entertainment and as a repository of Welsh heroic tradition.

### The Tasks and Formulaic Structure
When the party reaches Ysbaddaden's fortress, the giant sets "forty seemingly impossible tasks in return for the hand of Olwen". The presentation follows a strict formulaic pattern: upon hearing each task, "Culhwch replies, 'It is easy for me to get that, though you may think it's not easy', to which the giant retorts, 'Though you may get that, there is something you will not get', and proceeds to describe the next challenge". This repetitive structure creates "a chorus of sorts between the naming of each task, easing the listing, and also the listening, process".

### The Hunt for Twrch Trwyth
The most prominent and extensively developed task involves hunting Twrch Trwyth, a supernatural boar carrying the necessary grooming implements between his ears. The hunt begins in Ireland and moves across South Wales, with Arthur personally leading the final assault. During the climactic battle in the river Hafren (Severn), multiple warriors work together: "Mabon son of Modron spurred his horse on the one side and grabbed the razor from him, and on the other side Cyledyr Wyllt rushed into the Hafren on another horse and snatched the shears from him". The retrieval of these items comes at great cost, with many warriors drowning or being killed.

### Structural and Narrative Analysis  
The tale exhibits structural imbalances that have puzzled scholars: "of the forty tasks that are set by the giant Ysbaddaden, only twenty-one are accomplished". Some scholars suggest this may reflect the tale's composite nature, with "the male and female protagonists––the stereotyped Culhwch and Olwen––" possibly being "a deliberate creation on the part of the author as a vehicle to bring together numerous episodes that were already in circulation concerning Arthur and the Arthurian world".
