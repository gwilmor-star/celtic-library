Culhwch and Olwen (also spelled Culhwch ac Olwen) is the oldest surviving Arthurian tale in Welsh literature, dating from around 1100 AD, which tells the story of a young man's quest to win the hand of a giant's daughter through completing seemingly impossible tasks.

## Key Facts

- Culhwch receives his name from being born in a pig-run (hwch means 'pig' in Welsh), though the etymology may also relate to 'slender'.
- The tale involves forty tasks set by the giant Ysbaddaden Bencawr, though only a fraction are narratively completed in the surviving version (the text addresses approximately 10–14 tasks in detail, leaving the rest unresolved).
- Arthur and his warriors, including Cai and Bedwyr, assist Culhwch in accomplishing the tasks.
- The central quest involves hunting the magical boar Twrch Trwyth to obtain a comb and scissors from between its ears.
- The story culminates when Goreu son of Custennin kills Ysbaddaden and Culhwch marries Olwen.

## Q&A Sections

### What is the basic plot of Culhwch and Olwen?
The tale begins when Culhwch's stepmother places a curse on him that he will marry no one except Olwen, daughter of Ysbaddaden Chief Giant. Upon hearing Olwen's name, Culhwch immediately falls in love and seeks help at Arthur's court. Six warriors are assigned as his helpers, and they journey to Ysbaddaden's fortress where the giant sets forty seemingly impossible tasks that must be completed before Culhwch can marry his daughter.

### What are the main tasks that Culhwch must complete?
The forty tasks include providing food, drink, and entertainment for the wedding feast, but many focus on hunting Twrch Trwyth, a magical boar who carries a comb and scissors between his ears that Ysbaddaden requires to trim his hair for the wedding. The hunting of this supernatural creature forms the central adventure, with Arthur and his men pursuing the boar from Ireland across South Wales to Cornwall.

### Who are the key characters in the story?
Besides the protagonists Culhwch and Olwen, the tale features Arthur as the helpful king, along with his warriors Cai and Bedwyr. Ysbaddaden Bencawr serves as the antagonistic giant father, while Goreu son of Custennin ultimately kills the giant. The story includes a famous list of some 260 characters present at Arthur's court, drawn from various legendary and historical sources.

### How does the story end?
After accomplishing the required tasks, including the successful hunt of Twrch Trwyth where the comb and scissors are obtained from the boar in the river Hafren, Goreu son of Custennin grabs Ysbaddaden by the hair, drags him to a mound, cuts off his head, and takes possession of his fortress. Culhwch then sleeps with Olwen, who becomes his only wife for the rest of his life, and Arthur's men disperse to their respective countries.

## In-Depth Overview

### Literary Structure and Composition
Culhwch and Olwen represents a unique form within Welsh literature, structured around elaborate rhetorical passages and formulaic exchanges. The dialogue between Ysbaddaden and Culhwch follows a repetitive pattern where the giant challenges Culhwch to perform each task, Culhwch replies "It is easy for me to get that, though you may think it's not easy," and the giant retorts "Though you may get that, there is something you will not get" before describing the next challenge. This formulaic structure suggests the tale's origins in oral performance tradition.

### The Court of Arthur
The tale presents Arthur's court at Celli Wig in Cornwall as the gathering place for an extraordinary assembly of warriors. The famous list includes characters with descriptive names such as Canhastyr Hundred-Hands, Sgilti Lightfoot, and Gwiawn Cat-Eye, reflecting both the fantastical nature of the narrative and its roots in earlier Welsh heroic tradition. Among the courtiers, Gwalchmei (Gawain) is identified as Arthur's nephew and one of his "Six Helpers" appointed to assist in the quest for Olwen.

### The Hunt of Twrch Trwyth
The pursuit of the magical boar Twrch Trwyth forms the narrative centerpiece, with references to this creature appearing in the ninth-century History of the Britons, indicating the antiquity of this particular tradition. The hunt sequence reads like "a fast-moving film" as Arthur and his warriors chase the boar and his piglets from Ireland across South Wales. The climactic battle occurs in the river Hafren where Mabon son of Modron grabs the razor, Cyledyr Wyllt snatches the shears, and they struggle to obtain the comb before the boar reaches Cornwall.

### Oral Performance Tradition
The tale demonstrates clear markers of oral composition, particularly in its rhetorical set-pieces and the rhythmical description of Culhwch's equipment. These passages echo the elaborate style of medieval Welsh court poets and are designed for public declamation rather than silent reading. The two major lists in the tale—the roll-call of Arthur's court and the enumeration of tasks—rely heavily on alliteration and would have been performed aloud for maximum effect.

### Historical and Mythological Context
The tale merges early Celtic mythological elements with legendary heroes from Wales and North Britain, creating associations between protagonists from unrelated narrative cycles. This synthesis reflects a late stage in the development of heroic stories, where careful preservation of bardic tradition allowed diverse material to be assembled at Arthur's court. The redaction of Culhwch in its present form occurred around 1100 AD, approximately fifty years before Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae drew upon similar traditional sources.
