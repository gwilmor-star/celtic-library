The Dagda is a major deity of the Tuath Dé (Tuatha Dé Danann) in Irish mythology, known as the "good god" (from Irish *dag* "good" + *dia* "god") who controlled weather, crops, and performed miracles for his people.

## Key Facts

- The Dagda was called Eochaid Ollathair ("Great Father") and earned the title "Dagda" meaning "good god" for working wonders and controlling storms and harvests.
- He possessed two magical attributes: a cauldron of plenty that never emptied and a massive club that could kill the living and resurrect the dead.
- In *Cath Maige Tuired*, he was forced by the Fomorians to consume an enormous vat of porridge from a hole in the ground as a test of endurance.
- He had sexual unions with both the Mórrigan before the Second Battle of Mag Tuired and with Boand (Étaín), fathering the god Óengus Mac Óc.
- Despite his divine powers, the Dagda was consistently portrayed as gross, uncouth, and physically grotesque in Irish literature.

## Q&A Sections

### What was the Dagda's role in the Second Battle of Mag Tuired?

The Dagda played a crucial role as both negotiator and warrior in the conflict against the Fomorians. He was sent to the enemy camp to arrange a truce, where the Fomorians mocked him by forcing him to consume a massive vat of porridge mixed with goats, sheep, and swine. Before the battle, he encountered the Mórrigan at the river Unius, where their sexual union ensured victory for the Tuath Dé. During the war council, when other gods boasted of their individual powers, the Dagda declared he could wield all their abilities himself, prompting the assembly to confirm his title "Dagda" (good hand).

### How did the Dagda acquire his magical attributes?

The Dagda possessed two legendary items that defined his power and character. His club was so enormous it had to be dragged on wheels, and it possessed the dual power to kill the living with one end and resurrect the dead with the other. His cauldron of plenty never ran empty and could feed any number of people. These attributes led early modern scholars to compare him with Heracles and the Gaulish deity Sucellus. The magical harp Uaitne (also called Daur Dá Bláo, "Oak of Two Meadows," and Cóir Cetharchair, "Four-Angled Music") also belonged to him, containing bound melodies that would only sound when he called them forth with specific incantations.

### What was the Dagda's relationship with Óengus Mac Óc?

The Dagda was the father of Óengus Mac Óc through his affair with Boand, wife of Elcmar of the Bruig (Newgrange). To accomplish this union, the Dagda sent Elcmar on an errand and worked powerful spells to make nine months pass as a single day for the absent husband. When Óengus later sought territory from his father, the Dagda claimed he had nothing left to give. However, Óengus cleverly asked for "just a day and a night" in the Dagda's dwelling, then argued that since "the whole world is day and night," he had been granted permanent possession. This clever wordplay allowed Óengus to claim Newgrange as his own síd.

### How was the Dagda depicted during the reign of Bres?

Under the oppressive rule of Bres, the Dagda was reduced to manual labor as a rath-builder, forced to dig trenches around Rath Brese. During this period, he was tormented by a satirist named Cridenbél, who demanded the three best portions of the Dagda's daily ration. These portions were enormous—each the size of a good pig—and constituted a third of the Dagda's food, weakening his health. The situation was resolved when the Mac Óc helped the Dagda trick Cridenbél into swallowing three gold coins, which killed the lampooner when they were discovered in his stomach during an investigation.

## In-Depth Overview

### Divine Authority and Titles

The Dagda held the title Eochaid Ollathair, meaning "Great Father," reflecting his patriarchal status among the Tuath Dé. His more common epithet "Dagda" derives from the Irish words meaning "good god," earned through his ability to work miracles and control natural phenomena. In the context of Indo-European mythology, scholars following Georges Dumézil's trifunctional theory have positioned the Dagda as representing the third social function of wealth and food production. His omniscience and association with the cauldron of plenty have also led to identification with Celtic Otherworld deities, connecting him to figures like Donn and Dís Pater.

### Physical Characterization and Sexuality

Despite his divine status, the Dagda was consistently portrayed as physically grotesque and socially uncouth in Irish literature. The most vivid description appears in *Cath Maige Tuired*, where after consuming the Fomorians' porridge challenge, he travels with his penis exposed and his enormous belly impeding his movement. This deliberate contrast between divine power and crude physicality appears to be a distinctive feature of his characterization. His sexual encounters were equally prominent in the mythology—his unions with both the Mórrigan and Boand were crucial to mythological narratives, with the former ensuring military victory and the latter producing the important deity Óengus.

### Magical Implements and Powers

The Dagda's supernatural abilities were manifested through specific magical objects that became central to his identity. His club required wheels for transport due to its massive size and possessed the remarkable dual function of dealing death and restoring life. His cauldron of plenty ensured that no one left his hospitality hungry, reflecting his role as a provider deity. The magical harp Uaitne, with its two names Daur Dá Bláo ("Oak of Two Meadows") and Cóir Cetharchair ("Four-Angled Music") — these are the harp's names, not the club's, contained bound melodies that only responded to the Dagda's summoning calls. When the Fomorians stole this harp, the Dagda's retrieval of it demonstrated his mastery over the three musical strains that defined harper's art: sleep-strain, smile-strain, and wail-strain.

### Territorial Associations and Síd Distribution

The Dagda played a central role in the division of the síd (Otherworld mounds) among the Tuath Dé following their retreat from mortal Ireland. Initially, he retained Síd Lethet Lachmnaige and other territories including Cnoc Baine and Bró Ruair for himself. However, the most significant of his holdings was Síd in Broga (Newgrange), which he lost to his son Óengus through the latter's clever manipulation of language. This transfer established Newgrange as Óengus's eternal dwelling, described as a wondrous land with three perpetually fruit-bearing trees, an ever-living pig, a cooked pig, and a vessel of excellent liquor that never diminished.

## Primary Sources

***Cath Maige Tuired*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> Said the Dagdae: 'The power which ye boast I shall wield it all by myself.' 'It is thou art the Dagdae (good hand)' saith everyone: wherefore thenceforward the name 'Dagdae' adhered to him.

***Cath Maige Tuired*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> So the Dagdae went to the camp of the Fomorians and asked them for a truce of battle. [...] They fill for him the king's caldron, five fists deep, into which went four-score gallons of new milk and the like quantity of meal and fat. Goats and sheep and swine are put into it, and they are all boiled together with the porridge.

***Cath Maige Tuired*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> He beheld the woman in Unius in Corann, washing herself, with one of her two feet at Allod Echae i. e. Echumech, to the south of the water, and the other at Loscuinn, to the north of the water. Nine loosened tresses were on her head. The Dagdae conversed with her, and they make a union. 'The Bed of the Couple' is the name of the stead thenceforward.

***Tochmarc Étaíne*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Bergin & Best, Ériu 12 (1938)*
> There was a famous king of Ireland of the race of the Tuatha Dé, Eochaid Ollathair his name. He was also named the Dagda i.e. good god, for it was he that used to work wonders for them and control the weather and the crops.

***De Gabail in t-Síd*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Koch & Carey, Celtic Studies Publications (2003)*
> The Mac Óc came to the Dagda seeking territory when he had made the division to everyone [...] 'Obtain for me then,' said the Mac Óc, 'just a day and a night in your own dwelling.' [...] 'It is plain,' he said, 'that the whole world is day and night, and that is what was granted to me.'

***Cath Maige Tuired*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> Then the harp went forth from the wall, and kills nine men, and came to the Dagdae. And he played for them the three things whereby harpers are distinguished, to wit, sleep-strain and smile-strain and wail-strain.

***Cath Maige Tuired*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> The champions were also reduced to his service, to wit, Ogma had to carry a bundle of firewood, and the Dagdae was a rath-builder, wherefore he, the Dagdae, trenched Rath Brese.
