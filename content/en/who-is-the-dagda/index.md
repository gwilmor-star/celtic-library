The Dagda is a major deity of the Tuatha Dé Danann in Irish mythology, known as "the good god" who controlled weather, crops, and worked wonders for his people. He appears prominently in medieval Irish tales as both a powerful divine king and a father figure among the gods.

## Key Facts

- The Dagda's name means "good god" and he was also called Eochaid Ollathair, working miracles and controlling weather and crops for the Tuatha Dé Danann.
- He possessed a magical harp named Daurdabla ("Oak of two greens") and Coir-cethar-chuir ("Four-angled music") that could play sleep-strain, smile-strain, and wail-strain.
- The Dagda fathered Aengus (the Mac Óc) through a liaison with Boann, wife of Elcmar, using magic to make nine months pass as one day.
- He served as a forced laborer under the Fomorian-backed king Bres, building trenches and fortifications while being exploited by the satirist Cridenbel.
- The Dagda divided Ireland among the Tuatha Dé Danann after their rule, allocating territories and síde (otherworld mounds) to various divine figures.

## Q&A Sections

### What does the name "Dagda" mean and what were his main attributes?

The name "Dagda" means "good god," as explained in the Tochmarc Étaíne: "He was also named the Dagda i.e. good god, for it was he that used to work wonders for them and control the weather and the crops". His full name was Eochaid Ollathair. The Dagda gained his epithet during preparations for the Second Battle of Mag Tuired when he boasted he could wield all the powers that other warriors claimed individually, leading everyone to declare "It is thou art the Dagda (good hand)".

### Who were the Dagda's most important children and relationships?

The Dagda's most famous son was Aengus (the Mac Óc), born from his affair with Boann, wife of Elcmar of the Brugh. To conceal this liaison, the Dagda sent Elcmar away on errands and worked "great spells" to make nine months pass like a single day. Other sons included Aed, Cermait caem, and through various relationships he fathered multiple divine offspring. The Dagda also had connections with the war goddess known as the Morrígan, meeting her at the river Unius before the great battle.

### What role did the Dagda play during the Fomorian oppression?

Under the kingship of Bres, who was backed by Fomorian powers, the Dagda was reduced to manual labor as a "rath-builder" who "had to dig the trenches about Rath Brese". He was exploited by a satirist named Cridenbel, who demanded "the three best bits" of the Dagda's food ration each night, leaving the god weakened. The Mac Óc (Aengus) helped his father by providing three gold coins to place in Cridenbel's portion, which killed the satirist and freed the Dagda from this burden.

### What magical objects and powers did the Dagda possess?

The Dagda owned a magical harp with two names: "Daur-da-bla" meaning "Oak of two greens" and "Coir-cethar-chuir" meaning "Four-angled music". This harp could play three distinct musical strains that affected listeners emotionally and physically: "sleep-strain and smile-strain and wail-strain". When the Fomorians stole his harp, the Dagda retrieved it by calling its names, whereupon "the harp went forth from the wall, and killed nine men, and came to the Dagda". He also received a magical heifer as payment for his forced labor, which could call all the cattle of Ireland when it lowed for its calf.

### How did the Dagda distribute territories among the Tuatha Dé Danann?

As high king, the Dagda "divided Erin among the Tuatha Dé Danann" and "divided the síde among the Fir De ['men of the gods']". He allocated specific territories: "Lug son of Eithliu in Sid Rodruban, Ogma in Sid Aircheltrai, the Dagda himself however had Sid Lethet Lachtrnaige" and other locations. He gave "Mag Fliuchross to his son Aed". However, the Dagda famously lost his primary residence, Sid in Broga (Newgrange), to his son Aengus through a clever word-trick involving "a day and a night".

## In-Depth Overview

### Divine Kingship and Authority

The Dagda held supreme authority among the Tuatha Dé Danann as "a famous king of Ireland of the race of the Tuatha Dé". His royal title Eochaid Ollathair positioned him as the divine patriarch, while his epithet "Dagda" reflected his role as the benevolent provider who "used to work wonders for them and control the weather and the crops". This agricultural and meteorological control made him essential to his people's survival and prosperity.

The Dagda's authority extended beyond his own reign into the distribution of Ireland itself among the divine tribes. After the Tuatha Dé Danann's period of rule, "he divided Erin among the Tuatha Dé Danann" and specifically allocated the sacred síde (otherworld mounds) to different gods. This act of territorial distribution established lasting divine domains and reflected the Dagda's role as the ultimate arbiter of divine governance.

### Magical Abilities and Artifacts

The Dagda's supernatural powers manifested through both his personal abilities and his magical possessions. His most famous artifact was his harp, which bore the dual names "Daur-da-bla" ("Oak of two greens") and "Coir-cethar-chuir" ("Four-angled music"). This instrument possessed consciousness and loyalty, responding to the Dagda's summons even when stolen by enemies. When he called its names, "the harp went forth from the wall, and killed nine men, and came to the Dagda".

The harp's power lay in its ability to evoke specific emotional and physical responses through three distinct musical modes. The Dagda "played wail-strain to them, so that their tearful women wept. He played smile-strain to them, so their women and children laughed. He played sleep-strain to them, and the company fell asleep". This combination of entertainment and enchantment made the harp both a symbol of artistic mastery and a weapon of magical warfare.

### Relationships and Offspring

The Dagda's most significant romantic liaison involved Boann, the personification of the River Boyne and wife of Elcmar, lord of Brugh na Bóinne (Newgrange). Although "the woman would have yielded to the Dagda had it not been for fear of Elcmar, so great was his power", the Dagda overcame this obstacle through temporal magic. He "sent Elcmar away on a journey" and "worked great spells upon Elcmar as he set out, that he might not returns betimes". Through these enchantments, "nine months went by as one day" for Elcmar, allowing the Dagda's affair and the birth of their son Aengus to occur undetected.

The Dagda also had encounters with otherworldly beings, notably meeting "the Morrígan" at the river Unius before the Second Battle of Mag Tuired. This goddess appeared "washing herself, with one of her two feet at Allod Echae" on one side of the water and "the other at Loscuinn" on the other side. Their union at "The Bed of the Couple" resulted in the Morrígan providing strategic intelligence about Fomorian movements and promising to assist in the coming battle.

### Period of Subjugation

During the reign of Bres, a king with Fomorian backing, the Dagda experienced a dramatic reversal of fortune that demonstrated both divine vulnerability and resilience. The formerly supreme deity was "reduced to their service" and became "a rath-builder, and had to dig the trenches about Rath Brese". This forced labor represented a complete inversion of the natural order, with the god of abundance and prosperity reduced to manual construction work.

The Dagda's situation worsened through the exploitation of Cridenbel, "an idle blind man" and satirist who demanded "the three best bits" of the Dagda's food ration each night. These portions were substantial - "the size of a good pig" - and constituted "a third of the Dagda's ration," leaving the god physically weakened. The Mac Óc (Aengus) eventually freed his father through a clever trick involving three gold coins that killed the parasitic satirist when consumed.

### Battle Leadership and Recovery

The Dagda played a crucial role in the preparations for the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, the conflict that would restore the Tuatha Dé Danann to power. When various warriors and druids boasted of their individual contributions to the coming battle, the Dagda declared: "The power which ye boast I shall wield it all by myself". This comprehensive claim to all martial and magical abilities earned him renewed recognition as "the Dagda (good hand)".

Following the successful battle, the Dagda participated in pursuing the defeated Fomorians to recover his stolen harp, demonstrating both the recovery of divine prerogatives and the restoration of cosmic order. His magical heifer, received as payment for his forced labor, proved instrumental in the aftermath by calling "all the cattle of Ireland which the Fomorians had taken as their tribute" back to their rightful owners.
