The Celtic Otherworld is another world that sits right alongside our own. Not up in the sky, not down in the ground, but next door: always there, just out of sight. And it is not usually the land of the dead, although at times it stands in for that too. In the old Irish and Welsh stories it goes by many names, each one catching a different side of the place, and the beings who live there are eternal souls and gods, not ghosts. What pulls people across to it, and what they carry home from it, tells us as much about everyday life as it does about the world beyond.

## 1. What are the names of the Celtic Otherworld and where is it?

The Otherworld has no fixed location and no single name. In Ireland it goes by a handful of titles, sometimes naming a different good thing about the place, while in Wales it is usually called Annwfn. Sharon Paice Macleod lays out some of the meanings:

> 'In Celtic tradition, the realms of the Áes Síde had a variety of indigenous names. In Ireland the Otherworld consisted of a number of supernatural kingdoms, including Tír na nÓg (the "Land of Youth"), Mag Mell ("The Plain of Honey"), Tír Tairngiri ("The Land of Promise") and Mag Argatnél ("The Plain of Silver Clouds"). In Welsh tradition the Otherworld was called Annwfn, a word that was later shortened to Annwn. This term means either the "Not-World" (i.e., the "Other" World), or the "Very Deep World" (referring more specifically to the Underworld).' (1)

So each name points at something different: youth, sweetness, a promise kept, or sheer depth. The Welsh word Annwfn may be related to an old Gaulish word for the deep places under the earth. If so, the idea is genuinely ancient: not something the medieval writers dreamed up. Then again Annwfn could also imply a deeper dimension, not necessarily below ground, but underneath everything.

Was the Irish Otherworld ever thought of as one single place, or many separate kingdoms?

Scholars still argue about it, and the old texts don't give a clear answer. As Angelika Rüdiger points out:

> 'To the question: "Where is the Other World?", "Is it one or many?", the answers furnished by myth are contradictory. It is the "lower" half of Ireland, the land under the earth or the síd-mounds. It is also "the land under the wave" an island, or a series of islands beyond the sea. Yet it can manifest itself in other places […]. The limits of such a world cannot be "defined" in terms of distance and direction.' (2)

The Welsh Annwfn hangs together as one thing more readily than any single Irish name does. No Irish word seems to have worked as a single catch-all the way Annwfn does in Welsh.

## 2. Who lives in the Celtic Otherworld?

The Otherworld is home to the aes síde, the "People of the Síd", who are Ireland's older gods. They are the Tuatha Dé Danann, who withdrew into the sacred mounds after mortal Gaels defeated them. These mounds are real places you can still visit. Macleod notes that they are "the burial mounds of the earlier Neolithic inhabitants of Ireland, whose sanctity was apparently recognized and woven into the mythology of Celtic-speaking Gaels ... " (3) Ancient stone monuments, already old, got turned into doorways to the Otherworld: a story of place laid down over real archaeology.

An early Old Irish poem, as Macleod sums it up, describes them as something far grander and more dangerous than the English term 'fairy' conjures:

> 'They carry white shields in their hands decorated with emblems of pale silver, with glittering blue-grey swords and mighty horns. A pale-visaged and fair-haired supernatural assembly, they march ahead of their chieftain, scattering their foes. The Áes Síde are described as having smooth bodies with bright eyes, pure shining teeth and thin red lips. They are said to possess great strength, and are experts in battle and at playing fidchell … In addition, they are described as being melodious in the mead hall and masterful at making songs.' (4)

Warriors, musicians, masters of the board game. The aes síde are full-blooded powers: gods under another name.

## 3. What is the Celtic Otherworld like?

The Otherworld is not just somewhere else: it is better. Again and again the old Irish stories have an Otherworld being turn up and invite a mortal across, describing the place in a way that makes our own world sound poor and shabby by comparison. One early Irish poem gives such an invitation:

> 'Blessed friend, will you come with me, to a land in which music is found? Hair is like the blooming primrose there, and the colour of snow is every body. There is neither "mine" nor "yours" here, teeth are bright, and brows are dark / A delight to the eye is our host, the colour of foxglove is every cheek.' (5)

No "mine" and "yours." Beauty that never fades. Nothing rots or wears out. These are the things the Otherworld always promises. "Trees bore blossom and fruit at the same time there; no one ever aged or grew infirm; death had no dominion in the Otherworld." (6) This isn't heaven in the Christian sense, though later on the two would bleed together. Before that happened, it was simpler than that: the everyday world, only put right.

## 4. How do you get into the Celtic Otherworld?

You get in through ordinary places: water, woods, caves, burial mounds. The Otherworld sits right next to ours all the time, so there's no need to travel far. Macleod says:

> 'One of the most pervasive themes seen throughout the Celtic tradition is the belief that the Otherworld — the realms of the divine — exist around us at all times. The Otherworld was a contiguous realm to our own, one that was always connected to our world, existing in a different plane of space and time.' (7)

This changes how the crossings work. You don't need a grand quest to get there. People meet the Otherworld in plain landscapes: by a river, in a forest, beside a mound. Certain nights, Samain (1 November) and Beltaine (1 May), make the wall between worlds thinner, but Macleod is clear that crossings "could occur almost anywhere and at any time." (8)

Monaghan lists the ways people end up there: some wander in by accident, some are invited, some are snatched, some are lured across by a fairy lover. (6) The Otherworld doesn't sit and wait to be found. It can come looking for you.

**Deeper reading:** Notice what counts as a doorway: water, caves, woods, and the old burial mounds. These are the edges of the everyday world, the places where the solid ground of normal life runs out. The tradition put its doors exactly where our world already feels uncertain, and it set the open nights at the turn of summer and the turn of winter, the hinges of the year. The thinking underneath is consistent: you cross over not by going far, but by reaching the point where one thing stops and another begins. The Otherworld is pressed up against every edge and limit of our world.

## 5. Why do kings visit the Celtic Otherworld?

Kings go to the Otherworld to come back with the right to rule. The journey gives the king his authority and proves the whole order of things is on his side. When Conn Cétchathach or Cormac mac Airt makes the journey, what he carries home is power, gifts with strange properties, and a kind of cosmic stamp of approval on his kingship.

Time runs differently over there, and people seem to have taken this seriously, not just as a story. Macleod points to a striking scrap of church history: in 748 CE, Pope Zachary wrote to Saint Boniface about an Irish churchman named Virgil, who was telling his Bavarian congregation "that there was another world and other men beneath the earth, as well as a sun and moon." (9) The fact that the Church bothered to condemn it tells us that people actually believed it.

**Deeper reading:** Put the king's journey next to the cauldron in the Welsh raid (section 6) and a single rule shows through. The Otherworld doesn't just hand out its gifts. The cauldron warms for a brave man and won't cook a coward's food; the king has to make the crossing in person before he's confirmed. In both cases the Otherworld is the judge, not a pretty destination. It tests who stands in front of it and answers accordingly. Kingship, in this way of thinking, isn't something you simply hold but something the other world keeps checking you still deserve.

## 6. What is Annwn in Welsh mythology?

Annwn (or more formally Annwfn) is the Welsh name for the Otherworld. The Mabinogi cycle begins off with a journey there. The First Branch opens with Pwyll, prince of Dyfed, blundering onto a hunt that belongs to Arawn, king of Annwfn. "They exchange places for one year during which time Pwyll kills Arawn's enemy — an example of an Otherworld figure invoking the help of a mortal." (10) A mortal swapping places with the king of the Otherworld is the founding act of the entire cycle.

The poem Preiddiau Annwfn, in the Book of Taliesin, goes further still: Arthur himself leads a raid on the fortresses of Annwfn to steal a magic cauldron. It goes terribly: most of his company die, and the cauldron has a will of its own, warming only for the brave and refusing to cook a coward's food. This echoes the kingship themes from the Irish stories. As Carla Jefferson puts it, Annwfn "may be coterminous with the real world, or in another plane, and is accessible through mounds, caves, water, and other portals." (11)

## 7. How did Christianity change the Celtic Otherworld?

Christianity didn't wipe out the Otherworld, but took it in and gave it a new home. The old western paradise turned up again in the imram, the voyage tale: the pagan islands of plenty were reread as earthly paradises set aside for the faithful. The most famous of these is the Voyage of Brendan. And the Otherworld proved remarkably hard to shake, even in stories that were trying to be Christian or to pass as history. It lingers right across the Arthurian material, early and late: "the Otherworld has been a constant feature. Even in the works purporting to be history, there is always an element of the Otherworld lurking somewhere." (12)

The Celtic Otherworld won't sit still in any one box, and that's the point of it. It's next door yet out of reach, full of plenty yet deadly, a place of both beauty and terror. It is by its nature paradoxical. Its many names aren't really synonyms; they're different faces of the same thing. And the tensions it never resolves are exactly what have kept it alive across twelve hundred years of storytelling: one place or many, under the earth or out past the waves, pagan paradise or Christian promise.

**Deeper reading:** The tradition never once tries to tidy itself up. Is it one realm or many? Underground or over the sea? It gives both answers and none. It's a place of plenty and a place that kills most of Arthur's men. Even Christianity, which might have flattened the whole thing, ends up absorbing the contradictions rather than ironing them out. The people who told these stories seem to have held that some real things genuinely are two things at once, and that forcing them into a single shape would be the lie. The Otherworld stays alive precisely because no one ever made it make sense.
