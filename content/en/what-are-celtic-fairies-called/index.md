Celtic fairies are known by different names across the Celtic nations, including *sídhe* in Irish, *Daoine Sìth* in Scottish Gaelic, *Tylwyth Teg* in Welsh, and variations such as *pixies* in Cornwall. These supernatural beings represent one of the most enduring aspects of Celtic folklore and mythology.

## Key Facts

- The Irish term *sídhe* refers to fairy beings who dwell in burial mounds and otherworldly realms.
- Welsh fairies are called *Tylwyth Teg*, meaning "fair folk" or "fair family".
- Scottish Gaelic uses *Daoine Sìth* for fairy folk, translated as "People of Peace" or "Silent Moving Folk".
- Celtic fairies are reportedly associated with the Tuatha Dé Danann, though the provided sources do not confirm details about their retreat to the otherworld (sources limited).
- These beings inhabit various supernatural realms accessible through natural features like lakes, mounds, and stone circles.

## Q&A Sections

### What do the different Celtic names for fairies mean?
The names reflect the reverence and caution with which Celtic peoples approached these supernatural beings. The Irish *sídhe* derives from the burial mounds they inhabit, while Scottish *Daoine Sìth* means "People of Peace". Welsh *Tylwyth Teg* translates as "fair folk" or "fair family," emphasizing their beauty and otherworldly nature. These euphemistic names demonstrate the Celtic practice of speaking respectfully about powerful supernatural entities.

### How are Celtic fairies classified across different regions?
Welsh fairies are divided into five main categories: Ellyllon (elves), Coblynau (mine fairies), Bwbachod (household fairies), Gwragedd Annwn (lake and stream fairies), and Gwyllion (mountain fairies). Irish tradition speaks of various types including leprechauns and the *luchorpáin* (little bodies). Scottish fairies include the *Daoine Maithe* (Good People) and various Highland spirits. Each region developed its own taxonomy reflecting local geographical and cultural features.

### What is the relationship between Celtic fairies and the otherworld?
Celtic fairies are intrinsically connected to the otherworld, accessible through *sídhe* (fairy mounds) and natural portals. These beings are said to represent a continuation of ancient Celtic deities, though the sources do not confirm specific details about their retreat when Christianity arrived (sources limited). They maintain parallel societies beneath hills, under lakes, and in remote wilderness areas. Their realm exists alongside but separate from the human world, with specific times and places where the boundaries become permeable.

### How do Celtic fairy beliefs compare to broader European traditions?
Celtic fairy traditions share similarities with Germanic elves, Scandinavian trolls, and other European supernatural beings, suggesting common Indo-European roots. The Celtic Fairy-Faith represents a specialized form of universal human belief in spiritual realms inhabited by non-human intelligences. Medieval and modern occult traditions describe similar beings, termed "elementals" by Paracelsus, which correspond closely to Celtic fairy classifications. This suggests either common origins or parallel development of supernatural beliefs across cultures.

## In-Depth Overview

### Origins and Ancient Connections
Celtic fairy traditions represent the survival of pre-Christian religious beliefs transformed through centuries of cultural change. These supernatural beings likely originated as local deities, nature spirits, and ancestral figures who were gradually diminished in status as Christianity spread throughout Celtic lands. The connection between fairies and the Tuatha Dé Danann is said to demonstrate this evolution, though the sources do not confirm details about how ancient gods became fairy folk dwelling in *sídhe* mounds (sources limited). Archaeological evidence reportedly suggests that fairy mounds often correspond to actual prehistoric burial sites, indicating continuity between ancient sacred spaces and later folklore traditions (sources limited).

### Regional Variations and Names
Each Celtic region developed distinctive fairy traditions reflecting local geography and culture. In Ireland, the *sídhe* represent the most sophisticated fairy mythology, with complex social structures and clear connections to pre-Christian deities. Scottish traditions speak of *Daoine Sìth* dwelling in Highland fastnesses and green knolls. Welsh *Tylwyth Teg* are often associated with lakes and mountains, particularly around sacred sites like stone circles. Cornish pixies and Breton *fées* complete the Celtic fairy spectrum, each adapted to local landscapes and cultural memories.

### Physical Characteristics and Abilities
Celtic fairies display remarkable variability in size and appearance, from tiny beings small as children to figures of human stature or larger. Irish *leprechauns* and *luchorpáin* represent the diminutive tradition, often described as industrious shoemakers. Welsh accounts describe fairies riding miniature white horses "not bigger than dogs". Scottish traditions emphasize their otherworldly beauty and supernatural abilities, including shapeshifting and invisibility. These beings possess keen hearing, can travel at extraordinary speeds, and maintain elaborate underground societies with their own customs and hierarchies.

### Interaction with Human World
Celtic fairies actively engage with human communities through various forms of contact, both beneficial and potentially dangerous. They frequently borrow household items from farms, particularly around Snowdon where they emerge on misty days. Child abduction represents one of their most feared activities, with parents warned to keep children close during certain weather conditions. They also serve as guardians of ancient sites, with offerings still placed at fairy locations to ensure safe passage. Their festivals coincide with traditional Celtic celebrations, particularly May Eve, Midsummer Eve, and November Eve, when their power peaks.

### Modern Survival and Scholarly Assessment
Despite centuries of Christianization and modern education, Celtic fairy beliefs persist among hundreds of thousands of adherents. Contemporary Celtic communities continue to acknowledge fairy presence through protective customs and respectful avoidance of known fairy sites. Even educated individuals in Dublin, Scotland, and other Celtic regions maintain belief in fairy reality as spiritual beings. This persistence suggests that fairy traditions fulfill psychological and cultural needs that transcend religious change, serving as repositories of ancient wisdom and connection to the land. Modern occult and theosophical movements have found remarkable parallels between Celtic fairy descriptions and their own observations of nature spirits, lending contemporary credibility to ancient traditions.

***Welsh Folk-Lore*** *(1896)*
