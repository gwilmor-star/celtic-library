Celtic fairies are known by many names across different Celtic regions, with the Irish *Sidhe* (pronounced "shee"), Welsh *Tylwyth Teg* ("Fair Folk"), Scottish *Sìth*, and Cornish Pixies being the most prominent designations.

## Key Facts
- The Irish call their fairy folk *Daoine Maithe* ("Good People") or *Sidhe*, while the Scots refer to them as "People of Peace" or "Still-Folk".
- Welsh fairies are called *Tylwyth Teg*, meaning "Fair-Family" or "Fair-Folk," sometimes extended to *Tylwyth Teg yn y Coed* ("Fair Family in the Wood").
- Celtic fairies can be divided into five main classes in Wales: Ellyllon (elves), Coblynau (mine fairies), Bwbachod (household fairies), Gwragedd Annwn (lake fairies), and Gwyllion (mountain fairies).
- These supernatural beings are described as having "bodies of an elastic half-material essence" that is "sufficiently ethereal not to be visible to the physical sight".

## Q&A Sections

### What are the main regional names for Celtic fairies?
Each Celtic region has distinct names for fairy folk. The Irish use *Sidhe* (also spelled *Sídhe*) for their primary fairy race, along with *Daoine Maithe* meaning "Good People". Scottish traditions speak of *Sìth* who "live in green knolls and in the mountain fastnesses of the Highlands," calling them "People of Peace," "Still-Folk," or "Silent Moving Folk". Welsh fairies are known as *Tylwyth Teg*, literally "Fair-Family" or "Fair-Folk," with variations like *Tylwyth Teg yn y Coed* ("Fair Family in the Wood"). Cornwall has its Pixies, while Brittany refers to *Fées* (fairies) and *Corrigans*.

### How are Celtic fairies categorized by type and function?
Welsh tradition divides fairies into five distinct classes based on their habitat and role: the Ellyllon or elves, the Coblynau or mine fairies who work underground, the Bwbachod or household fairies who interact with human homes, the Gwragedd Annwn who are fairies of lakes and streams, and the Gwyllion or mountain fairies. Irish folklore mentions specific types like the *lepra-caun* (leprechaun), described as "the shoemaker" who is "the only industrious person amongst them". These beings are characterized by their varied occupations of "feasting, fighting, and making love, and playing the most beautiful music".

### What physical characteristics distinguish Celtic fairies?
Celtic fairies are notable for their variable size and form. "Everything is capricious about them, even their size. They seem to take what size or shape pleases them". Many are described as small beings, with Irish traditions speaking of *luchorpáin* or "little bodies" and references to pygmy-like creatures. However, some testimony suggests that certain fairy races, particularly those identified with the Tuatha De Danann, "are much taller than our race". They are often depicted riding "little white horses, not bigger than dogs" and appearing in groups of hundreds.

### What is the spiritual nature of Celtic fairies according to traditional belief?
Celtic fairy belief represents "that specialized form of belief in a spiritual realm inhabited by spiritual beings which has existed from prehistoric times until now" across Celtic lands. These beings are understood to possess "bodies of an elastic half-material essence, which is sufficiently ethereal not to be visible to the physical sight, and probably comparable to matter in the form of invisible gases". The fairy faith is described as having "the same origin as all religions and mythologies," representing attempts to explain "an unseen world, peopled by unseen beings".

## In-Depth Overview

### Linguistic and Cultural Variations
The terminology for Celtic fairies reflects deep cultural and linguistic diversity across the Celtic regions. The Irish *Sidhe* derives from ancient Gaelic traditions and refers specifically to the fairy mounds or hills where these beings were believed to dwell. The respectful term *Daoine Maithe* ("Good People") demonstrates the careful diplomatic language used when speaking of these potentially powerful supernatural beings. Scottish usage of terms like "Still-Folk" or "Silent Moving Folk" emphasizes the mysterious, quiet nature attributed to these beings in Highland tradition.

Welsh *Tylwyth Teg* literally translates as "Fair-Family," indicating both their beauty and their organized social structure. The extended form *Tylwyth Teg yn y Coed* specifically locates them in woodland settings, reflecting the strong connection between Welsh fairies and natural environments. Cornish Pixies represent a distinct regional tradition, while Breton *Fées* and *Corrigans* demonstrate the continental Celtic fairy traditions.

### Classification Systems and Functional Roles
Traditional Welsh folklore provides the most systematic classification of fairy types. The five main categories each serve specific functions: Ellyllon represent the classic elven tradition, while Coblynau are specifically associated with mining regions and underground work. Bwbachod function as household spirits, interacting directly with human domestic life, while Gwragedd Annwn are water spirits associated with lakes and streams. Gwyllion inhabit mountainous regions and are often portrayed as more dangerous or unpredictable.

Irish traditions emphasize the social organization of fairy folk, describing their "chief occupations" as "feasting, fighting, and making love, and playing the most beautiful music". The leprechaun stands out as uniquely industrious among them, working as "the shoemaker" while other fairies engage in more leisurely pursuits. This suggests a complex fairy society with both working and aristocratic classes.

### Physical Manifestations and Abilities
The physical appearance of Celtic fairies defies simple categorization. Traditional accounts emphasize their shape-shifting abilities, noting that "everything is capricious about them, even their size. They seem to take what size or shape pleases them". While many are described as small or pygmy-like beings, with references to *luchorpáin* meaning "little bodies," other traditions suggest much larger fairy races.

Contemporary accounts describe fairies riding in organized groups, "mounted on little white horses, not bigger than dogs, and riding four abreast". Some witnesses reported seeing "fairies to the number of hundreds" in such processions. Battle scenes between fairy armies are also recorded, with "two armies, one of which was mounted on milk-white steeds, and the other on horses of jet-black" fighting "with the utmost fury".

### Metaphysical Nature and Spiritual Significance
Celtic fairy belief represents more than simple folklore; it constitutes "that specialized form of belief in a spiritual realm inhabited by spiritual beings" that has maintained continuity across centuries. These beings are understood to possess semi-material bodies described as "an elastic half-material essence, which is sufficiently ethereal not to be visible to the physical sight".

The relationship between fairy belief and broader spiritual traditions is complex. These beings are often "confused with the dead," and "striking likenesses constantly appearing in our evidence between the ordinary apparitional fairies and the ghosts of the dead show that there is often no essential and sometimes no distinguishable difference between these two orders of beings". This suggests that Celtic fairy belief encompasses both nature spirits and ancestral spirits within a unified cosmological framework.
