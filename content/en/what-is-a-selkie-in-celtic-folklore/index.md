A selkie is a seal that can turn into a person. In the coastal folklore of Scotland and Ireland, a selkie slips off its seal-skin to walk on land in human shape, and it has to return to the sea the moment that skin is found and taken back. It belongs to the wider Celtic family of shape-shifting fairy beings, and for hundreds of years it shaped the family trees, the taboos, and the fireside tales of people who lived by the sea. One thing sets the selkie apart from the other fairy figures. It's not only the trick with the skin, it's the feeling that runs through the tales: longing, being held somewhere against your will, and a love that can never quite join two worlds.

## 1. What is a selkie and where do the stories come from?

A selkie is a seal-person from the folklore of Scotland and Ireland: in the water it's a seal, and on land it can shed its skin and walk about as a human. The stories cluster along those coasts, and thickest of all on the northern islands: the Hebrides off western Scotland and Shetland up to the far north. The folklorist Patricia Monaghan sets out where the tradition lived and what it held:

> "seal (seal people, silkie, selkie, roam roane) Scottish and Irish folkloric figure. A sleek furred mammal that lives in cold seas, the seal is found along Ireland's west and northern coast (occasionally on the east coast, as near Skerries), in many coastal regions of Scotland and, most significantly, on the Scottish islands. The idea that seals are enchanted people (sometimes, fallen angels) is found in both lands, at times confused with MERMAID legends." (1)

It's easy to muddle selkies with mermaids. Both live on the line between sea and land. A mermaid is fixed as half-fish. A selkie switches the whole way. Monaghan notes that "the name silkie (or selkie) is also used for the apparently human SEAL race" (2). On land the seal-people are fully human. In the water they are fully seal.

## 2. How does a selkie change between seal and human?

A selkie changes by taking its seal-skin off and putting it back on. Everything turns on that skin, because a selkie can't get back to the sea without it, and whoever hides it holds the selkie on land. So, a man (usually) walking the shore at night finds a selkie dancing with her skin laid aside, snatches it, and hides it away. He gains a wife, not by winning her but by trapping her. Martina Maher says:

> "the selkie's seal coat is hidden by a human male, forcing her to stay on land. The selkie woman, who is now forced to stay ashore against her wishes, usually bears this man children but is unhappy because she is unable to return to the sea, which she longs to do. Later in the tale, when she finds her seal coat she returns to the sea, normally never to be seen again." (3)

Once she has the skin back, the marriage is over, every time. There's no telling of the tale where she gets the skin back and decides to stay.

This kind of story runs right along the coasts of seafaring Europe. Carl Lindahl sets the selkie in that wider pattern:

> "animals that mediate between elements or travel quickly are favored by shapeshifters, too: hence recurrent avian images or, among seafaring folk, marine mammals (cf. the selkies, or were-seals of the maritime Celtic fringe). Frequently a character dons a pelt and magically assumes the desired shape." (4)

The seal was an obvious choice for such a story. It moves between sea and land. Its eyes look startlingly human. And its calls can sound like someone crying.

## 3. What happens in a full selkie story?

In a full selkie story, a fisherman steals a seal-woman's skin, keeps her as his wife for years, and has a child with her, until she finds the skin and slips back to the sea. The story is really a story about force and what it costs. He steals the selkie's skin while she dances on the shore, and she stays with him for seven years and bears him a son. What she feels for him isn't plain hatred: she has a fondness and concern, but not really love. When she finally asks for her skin, his furious denial shows why this was never a real marriage:

His fury gives the game away. What looked like a marriage was something he never had her consent for. With her son's help she finds the skin and returns to the sea. But the story keeps one tender detail: she goes on leaving fish on the rocks for them, a sign that she still cares. The otherworld can't be tamed and kept for good. It doesn't simply vanish either.

**Deeper reading:** If you hide hide the skin, you hold the woman. Give it back and she's gone within the hour, for all that she's spent seven years as a wife and loves the son she's leaving behind. Her sea-nature was never a mood a kind husband could wear down. It was simply what she was. Her fondness for her human family, especially her children, is real to the last but changed nothing, because in these stories loving someone and belonging with them are two different things, and the sea always had the stronger pull.

## 4. Which families claimed to be descended from selkies?

A number of real coastal families in Ireland and Scotland claimed a seal ancestor far back in their line, among them the Sullivans in Ireland and the MacPhees in the Hebrides. For these families the selkie was a statement about who they were, and it carried a strict rule against ever killing or eating a seal. Monaghan gathers a number of these claims:

> "Some scholars remark upon the claim of seal ancestry by certain coastal families — in Ireland, the Coneelys, Flahertys, MacNamaras, Sullivans, and many families on Achill Island; on the Hebrides, the MacPhees; on the Scottish mainland, the MacCondrums; on the Isle of Skye, all fair-haired people. This may reveal an ancient totemic system; the taboo on members of such families killing seals or eating their meat points in the same direction." (5)

Sharon Paice Macleod backs this up: "In Ireland and Scotland, a number of families were believed to have connections with selkies (or to have been descended from a union between a human being and a seal)." (6) The rule against killing seals worked as a mark of kin, setting these families apart from their neighbours. And for people whose whole livelihood came from the sea, claiming a seal ancestor was a way of saying they belonged to the sea as much as to the land.

**Deeper reading:** Some families said a seal was their ancestor, and those same families would not kill a seal or eat its meat. Side by side, the rule looks less like kindness to animals and more like the old ban on eating your own kin. To a Sullivan or a MacPhee, the seal on the rocks was a relative, not a catch for the pot. That redraws the whole coastline. The sea stops being the wild edge of the human world and turns into a place where some of your family happen to live.

## 5. How are selkies related to swan maidens and fairy brides?

Selkies belong to a large family of stories in which a magical being in animal form is caught and married off to a mortal, and among them the swan maiden is the selkie's closest cousin. What links the whole group is one plot: a man wins a supernatural wife by seizing the thing that lets her change her shape. Sherman's encyclopaedia points to what makes selkies and swan maidens a type of their own within it:

> "Related to these tales are those of the swan maidens and Celtic selkies, or seal people. In these cases, the transformation into human form is not permanent; the being is able to switch back and forth between forms." (7)

The closest inland cousin comes from Wales, in its fairy-bride tales. The best known is the story of Llyn y Fan Fach (llyn simply means lake), where a woman rises out of the water, marries a farmer on set conditions, and leaves him once he breaks them. That has the same bones as a selkie story. But Juliette Wood points to one real difference:

> "The Welsh variants form a unique sub-group in that the woman is not in mermaid or animal shape and she sets the conditions under which she will remain (unlike the seal-woman common in Scotland and Scandinavia who is vulnerable by virtue of being out of her animal skin, or the Irish mermaid who loses some object)." (8)

In Wales the woman lays down her terms, but in the selkie tale she is simply taken. That gap in power shapes what each story is really about.

## 6. Are there selkie legends in Wales?

Not really, no. Wales has plenty of magical water-women, but they belong to its inland lakes and rivers rather than to seals, and the one Welsh figure people sometimes tie to a seal is Dylan Eil Ton, from the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi, the medieval Welsh tale-collection. The moment he's born he heads straight for the sea, and a few scholars have read that dash to the water as a sign of a seal father. But Juliette Wood looks hard at the idea and doesn't buy it. The heart of the case is a ballad from the Orkney Islands, written down in the nineteenth century, which some have used to build up the idea of one great seal-tale tradition shared across all the Celtic lands. Wood says that ballad is offered "with great authority but no substantiation" (9). She says: "the Welsh examples are in fact connected with inland lakes and rivers. Eventually she is forced to admit that 'there are no true seal-tales in Welsh.'" (10)

Selkie stories are coastal, grown out of living with real seals. They aren't an ancient birthright shared by every Celtic people.

**Deeper reading:** On the Atlantic islands, people saw seals hauling out on the rocks at dusk, with those strangely human eyes, and the tale grew of a stolen skin and a stolen wife. Around the lakes of Wales, where no seal ever came ashore, the same deep story kept going, but the woman is a lake-fairy who names her own terms and walks out when they're broken. The bones of the plot stayed the same. What changed was the location, and that, in the end, decided how the charcaters were imagined.

## 7. Why were seals linked to the Celtic Otherworld?

Because the sea itself was reckoned part of the otherworld, and the seal made its home right in that water. To the old Irish, the deep was the country of the sea-god Manannán mac Lir, so the animal you watched slip under the waves was, in the old picture, diving straight into the otherworld. A medieval Irish poem-collection called the Metrical Dindshenchas, which explains the lore behind place-names, puts the seals' home in the sea-god's domain:

> "eastward lies the seals' green plain, one of the Three great Moans of Mac Lir." (11)

So the seal-grounds sit inside the otherworldly sea, named in the poem itself. Sharon Paice Macleod says:

> "Seals play a prominent role in the folklore tradition, where they were noted for their remarkable humanlike expressions and actions. In some cases seals were believed to be supernatural creatures, or even human beings transformed into seal form. The Selkies are a well-known manifestation of these folklore beliefs. They were connected with transformation, shape shifting and the inhabitants of the watery Underworld realms." (12)

So the selkie sits at the very heart of this way of seeing the world, not off at its edges. It marks one of the places where the wall between our world and the otherworld is thin, made real in an animal that anyone on the Atlantic coast could watch on the rocks at dusk, its dark eyes turned toward the shore.

**Deeper reading:** A seal could be magical and transform into a person because the water it lived in was already the otherworld. So when an islander stood on the shore and met the steady gaze of a seal out on the swell, they were being watched from the very threshold of the otherworld, by something that might, on another night, walk up the beach and take off its skin.
