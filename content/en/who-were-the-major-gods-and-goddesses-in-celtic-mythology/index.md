The Tuath Dé (Tribes of the gods) were the primary divine beings of Irish mythology, forming a complex pantheon of gods and goddesses who ruled Ireland before the arrival of mortal peoples. These deities, later known as the Tuatha Dé Danann, represented various aspects of sovereignty, warfare, craftsmanship, and the natural world, with their stories preserved in medieval Irish literature.

## Key Facts

- The name Tuath Dé means "Tribe(s) of the gods" and appears in sources from the 11th century, with the variant Tuatha Dé Danann emerging in later medieval manuscripts.
- Ana or Danann was considered "the mother of the three chieftains of the Tuatha dó Danann, Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharbu, who were accounted gods for their feats of necromancy".
- Continental Celtic Gauls worshipped Mercury most of all their gods, followed by Apollo, Mars, Jupiter and Minerva, according to Julius Caesar's account.
- Celtic druids taught that "the spirit is eternal and another life awaits the spirits of the dead," leading warriors to fight more bravely.
- The gods were not confined to their own mythological cycle but "appear also in other cycles, where they impinge" on mortal heroes and events.

## Q&A Sections

### Who were the primary gods of the Irish pantheon?
The Irish pantheon centered on the Tuath Dé or Tuatha Dé Danann, divine beings who ruled Ireland in mythological times. Ana or Danann served as "the mother of the three chieftains of the Tuatha dó Danann, Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharbu, who were accounted gods for their feats of necromancy". The term "Tuath Dé" literally means "Tribe(s) of the gods" and first appears clearly in the verse of Eochaid ua Flainn around 1004 AD. These divine beings were distinguished from mortal heroes by their ability to appear across different narrative cycles and their supernatural powers.

### What gods did the Continental Celts worship?
According to Julius Caesar's account, the Gauls "worship Mercury most of all" their gods, considering him "the inventor of all the arts, the ruler of journeys and travelling" with "the greatest power over obtaining money and trade". After Mercury, they honored "Apollo, Mars, Jupiter and Minerva" with roles similar to Roman interpretations: "Apollo to drive away diseases, Minerva to teach the elements of skill, Jupiter to hold the order of the heavens, Mars to control wars". The Gauls also claimed descent from "the same father, Dis, and they say this has been transmitted from the Druids".

### How did Celtic religious beliefs influence warrior culture?
Celtic druids taught warriors that "the spirit is eternal and another life awaits the spirits of the dead" so "that warriors might fight more bravely". This belief led to practical customs where they would "burn or bury articles useful in life with the dead" and some would even "throw themselves willingly onto the funeral pyres of their relatives so that they ought live with them still". When deciding to fight battles, Celtic warriors would consecrate "a large part of the plunder" to Mars, and after victory would "collect captured living things and the rest and burn them in one place".

### What role did supernatural beings play in Celtic society?
The druids served as intermediaries between mortals and "the immortal gods," administering sacrifices because "unless for a man's life a man's life is given back, the will of the immortal gods cannot be placated". Celtic society was "completely devoted to religion," with those facing diseases or battle dangers either sacrificing "human victims or vow[ing] to do so using the Druids as administrators". The gods maintained active involvement in mortal affairs, with divine beings like Manannán mac Lir remembered as both historical figures and deities, as "Scoti et Brittones eum deum vocaverunt maris" (the Scots and Britons called him a god of the sea).

## In-Depth Overview

### The Irish Divine Pantheon
The medieval Irish sources present a sophisticated pantheon centered on the Tuath Dé, whose name evolution reflects changing religious perspectives. The earliest term "Tuath(a) Dé" meant "Tribe(s) of the gods," but medieval scribes expanded this to "Tuatha Dé Danann" partly to distinguish these pagan deities from the biblical "People of God". Ana or Danann emerges as a maternal figure for the divine triad of "Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharbu, who were accounted gods for their feats of necromancy". The Two Paps of Anu mountains in Kerry preserve the memory of the goddess Anu (also spelled Ana) — note that the Paps are associated with Anu, not Danu; the two names are related but treated as distinct in scholarship as landscape features associated with this goddess.

### Continental Celtic Divine Hierarchy  
Caesar's ethnographic account reveals a structured pantheon among the Gauls, with Mercury holding supremacy as patron of commerce, travel, and all arts. The Celtic interpretation of Roman divine equivalents shows sophisticated theological thinking: Apollo governed healing, Minerva taught crafts and skills, Jupiter maintained cosmic order, and Mars controlled warfare. This hierarchy reflects Celtic society's priorities, placing commercial and artistic innovation above pure military might, while maintaining Jupiter's role as cosmic sovereign.

### Druidic Cosmology and Afterlife Beliefs
Celtic druids developed complex teachings about divine nature and human destiny. They instructed nobles "in caves or hidden groves" for "up to twenty years" in subjects including "the movements of the heavens and stars, and the will of the gods". Their central doctrine proclaimed that "the spirit is eternal and another life awaits the spirits of the dead," making warriors fearless in battle. This belief system supported practices like including grave goods with the deceased and ritual self-sacrifice at funerals.

### Sacred Practices and Divine Interaction
Celtic religious practice emphasized direct communication with divine powers through druidic mediation. The druids administered human sacrifices because they believed "unless for a man's life a man's life is given back, the will of the immortal gods cannot be placated". They also constructed elaborate ritual offerings, creating "a huge figure of straw and wood" filled with "cattle and all manner of wild animals and humans" for burnt offerings. These practices reflected belief in divine beings who demanded reciprocal exchange with mortal society.

### Divine Intervention in Mortal Affairs
The gods maintained active presence in Celtic life through miraculous interventions and ongoing relationships with human communities. ## Primary Sources

***Cormac's Glossary (Sanas Cormaic)*** *(c. 900 AD)*
*transl. in: O'Donovan, John, transl.; Stokes, Whitley, ed. (1868)*
> Ana, or as she is most usually called Danann, was the mother of the three chieftains of the Tuatha dó Danann, Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharbu, who were accounted gods for their feats of necromancy. The "Two Paps", in the district of Luachair Deaghaidh in the County of Kerry, are two mountains, still so called, in the barony of Magunihy.

***Caesar's Gallic Wars*** *(c. 50 BC)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, eds. The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> Of the gods they worship Mercury most of all. Of him there are many images: they extol him as the inventor of all the arts, the ruler of journeys and travelling, and they judge him to have the greatest power over obtaining money and trade. After him Apollo, Mars, Jupiter and Minerva. Concerning these they have nearly the same opinions as the rest of people: Apollo to drive away diseases, Minerva to teach the elements of skill, Jupiter to hold the order of the heavens, Mars to control wars.

***Caesar's Gallic Wars*** *(c. 50 BC)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, eds. The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> The Gauls proclaim that they are all sprung from the same father, Dis, and they say this has been transmitted from the Druids.

***Pomponius Mela's De Situ Orbis*** *(c. 37-50 AD)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, eds. The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> One of their doctrines has become commonly known to the populace so that warriors might fight more bravely, that the spirit is eternal and another life awaits the spirits of the dead. Thus they burn or bury articles useful in life with the dead. For this reason also, in past times, they would defer business and payment of debts to the next life. There were some who would even throw themselves willingly onto the funeral pyres of their relatives so that they ought live with them still.

***Caesar's Gallic Wars*** *(c. 50 BC)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, eds. The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> All the people of Gaul are completely devoted to religion, and for this reason those who are greatly affected by diseases and in the dangers of battle either sacrifice human victims or vow to do so using the Druids as administrators to these sacrifices, since it is judged that unless for a man's life a man's life is given back, the will of the immortal gods cannot be placated.

***Strabo's Geography*** *(c. 7 BC - 23 AD)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, eds. The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> They would construct a huge figure of straw and wood, and having thrown cattle and all manner of wild animals and humans into it, they would make a burnt offering of the whole thing.

***Cormac's Glossary (Sanas Cormaic)*** *(c. 900 AD)*
*transl. in: O'Donovan, John, transl.; Stokes, Whitley, ed. (1868)*
> Manannán mac Lir, a celebrated merchant who was in the Isle of Mann. He was the best pilot that was in the west of Europe. He used to know by studying the heavens, the period which would be the fine weather and the bad weather, and when each of these two times would change. Inde Scoti et Brittones eum deum vocaverunt maris, et inde filium maris esse dixerunt i.e. mac lir 'son of sea'.

***Justin's Epitome*** *(c. 2nd-3rd century AD)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, eds. The Celtic Heroic Age (2003)*
> The temple priests and priestesses suddenly rushed to the front lines with their hair in disarray and wearing their holy insignia. They cried out that they had seen the god himself leaping down into his temple from the hole in the roof and that he was young and vigorous, with a beauty far beyond that of mortals. With him were two virgins in armour who had come to meet him from the nearby temples of Diana and Minerva.
