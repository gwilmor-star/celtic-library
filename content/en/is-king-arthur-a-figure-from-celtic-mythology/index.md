Yes. King Arthur is a figure of Celtic mythology, with his deepest roots in early Brythonic tradition: the world of the Celtic-speaking Britons who became the Welsh, the Cornish and the Bretons. People have argued for centuries about whether a real man stood behind the name, and that line between history and story has never been drawn cleanly. But look at the Arthur the Welsh actually remembered: a war-band leader, a raider of the Otherworld (the magical realm that runs alongside our own), and a king who would one day come back. Every one of those carries the marks of a Celtic hero. Whoever the real Arthur may have been, the figure who comes down to us is built out of Celtic myth. That is exactly why his beginnings are one of the most argued-over questions in the whole study of the Middle Ages.

## 1. Was King Arthur a real person or a myth?

Nobody can say for certain, and that is the honest answer. There may well have been a real war-leader behind the name, but the Arthur we can actually read about in the oldest sources is already a creature of legend.

The scholar Antone Minard says that Arthur is "a pseudohistorical king with mythological antecedents, the literature concerning him runs continuously from the early Welsh material to last year's miniseries Merlin." (1) "Pseudohistorical" means it's a story told like history without quite being it. The earliest sources we have, Gildas, the *Gododdin*, the *Historia Brittonum* (the "History of the Britons"), and the *Annales Cambriae* (the "Welsh Annals"), either say nothing about Arthur, say something too vague to pin down, or were written too late to count as proof on their own. Not one of them shows for certain that he ever lived.

And opinion has swung back and forth over the years. Sharon Paice Macleod traces the path: "In the early medieval period, Arthur's story formed an important part of British legendary tradition. In later centuries however, his physical existence came under scrutiny, and many postulated that he was not an historical figure at all. In more recent times, scholars have increasingly come to believe that the figure of Arthur does have some basis in history." (2) So where do scholars stand now? Not at either extreme. They neither confidently treat him as a flesh-and-blood king who was later puffed up into legend, nor write him off as pure invention. A real person behind the story is still possible. The layers of myth piled on top are beyond doubt.

## 2. What do the Welsh Triads say about King Arthur?

The Triads give us the earliest picture of Arthur in the Welsh language, and in them he is the high king of the whole island of Britain. He is no local chieftain here: he is the ruler the entire island looks to, set over all its other chieftains and princes.

The Triads, or *Trioedd Ynys Prydein* ("Triads of the Island of Britain"), are an old Welsh catalogue for storing lore in groups of three, a memory aid for the poets who kept the tradition alive in memory. They list Arthur as the chief ruler at three separate courts: "Arthur as Chief of Princes in Mynyw (St David's) … Arthur as Chief of Princes in Celliwig in Cornwall … Arthur as Chief of Princes in Pen Rhionydd in the North." (3) This grouping-by-threes was not a clever literary game someone dreamed up. It is how the tradition actually packaged and remembered itself. And the fact that Arthur sits inside it at this rank tells us he was already a fixed, central figure in learned Welsh culture long before Geoffrey of Monmouth ever wrote a word for his Norman patrons.

The Triads also hold the mythical bookends of his story. They remember Camlan, Arthur's last battle, where he fights Medrawd (the Mordred of the later romances). He is mortally wounded and laid to rest, in the Triads' words, "in a hall on the Island of Afallach" (4). Afallach is simply the Welsh name for Avalon here. Alwyn and Brinley Rees describe Arthur's pull in the wider tradition: "The brief references to Arthur in the work of Nennius and in several early Welsh poems show that he was a figure of renown in Welsh tradition at least from the eighth century… the Arthurian legend then becomes the dominant story tradition, a tradition which draws mythological personages from all types of story into King Arthur's entourage." (5) So even while the story was still purely Welsh, it was already doing what it would go on doing for centuries: gathering other heroes and otherworldly figures into Arthur's company. The Europe-wide craze came later.

## 3. Why is King Arthur considered a mythological figure and not just a war-leader?

Because the early Arthur does things no ordinary war-leader ever did. He is shut up in magic prisons and freed by magic, he crosses into the Otherworld, and his deadliest enemy commits a crime against the very idea of kingship.

Take his imprisonments. The Triads say he spent "three nights in prison in Caer Oeth and Anoeth, and three nights imprisoned by Gwen Pendragon, and three nights in an enchanted prison under the Rock of Echeifyeint." (6) A single unnamed lad is the one who sets him free. No chronicle records this. It is the stuff of Celtic Otherworld tales: the hero shut away and tested by powers from beyond the human world, then sprung loose.

The quarrel with Medrawd works the same way. What lights the fuse for the fatal battle of Camlan is an outrage against the king's hall and his wife. Medrawd "left neither food nor drink in the court that he did not consume. And he also dragged Gwenhwyfar from her royal chair, and then he struck a blow upon her." (7) Both the queen, Gwenhwyfar (Guinevere), and the open table of the court stood for the king's right to rule. To strike at them was to strike at the holy order of kingship, not merely to grab power.

Rachel Bromwich, one of the great scholars of early Welsh Arthur, says: "Whatever Arthur's ultimate origins may be, in early literature he belongs, like Fionn, to the realm of mythology rather than to that of history." (8) That nod to Fionn mac Cumhaill, the great hero of Irish legend, is worth following. Kevin Murray spots the same mythic shape in both figures. Arthur and his men, he writes, are "akin to the fíanna, bands of youths in Ireland who apparently used to roam the countryside, living temporarily or permanently outside normal society. The primary definition of early Irish fían perfectly describes Arthur and his followers as seen in this tale: 'a band of roving men whose principal occupations were hunting and war'." (9) A roving war-band, living outside the ordinary rules of settled life and bound to one magnetic leader is a pattern found right across the Celtic world and beyond into the deep Indo-European past.

**Deeper reading:** Notice what counts as the act of war here. Medrawd does not raise an army or seize a fortress. He eats the court bare and lays a hand on the queen. To us those look like private insults, the stuff of a family feud. To the early Welsh they were the wound itself. The king's wife, his open table, and the safety of his land all hung together as one fabric. Strike the queen and you had already struck the kingdom.

## 4. What Celtic myths and motifs are found in the King Arthur legend?

Several patterns in Arthur's tale come straight out of old Celtic mythic thinking. You can see them in what his story takes for granted, in his very name, and in the shape of his whole life.

Patricia Monaghan runs through them: "Among the mythological motifs that point to a Celtic origin for the tale is the emphasis on the rectitude of the king, upon which the land itself depends… Whether Arthur was originally a British or Welsh god, as suggested by his name's connection to the divine BEAR (in Welsh, *arth*), is arguable; some scholars find in the figure evidence of a Celtic (probably Brythonic) divinity, while others consider him a culture hero, perhaps based on an historical king." (10) "Rectitude" just means doing right, and the link it reveals, between a good king and a healthy land, runs through both Irish and Welsh story. As for the name: *arth* is simply the Welsh word for "bear," and *ur* probably means "golden" here. Nobody can prove whether that points to an old bear-god standing behind Arthur or is just a coincidence. Monaghan's other suggestion is that Arthur was a "culture hero": a legendary figure a people credit with shaping and guarding their whole way of life.

Sharon Paice Macleod paints that fuller picture: "Arthur was a tremendously important culture hero for the Celtic-speaking people in Britain (and other parts of the Celtic world). A brave and skillful battle leader, he helped the British Celts fight off invaders and protect their land, their culture and their traditions… His exploits eventually expanded to include adventures in the Celtic Otherworld realms, including his own journey to the famous Island of Avalon." (11) From a flesh-and-blood war-leader defending his people to a voyager into the Otherworld and on to Avalon. That climb, from earthly fighter to traveller in the magic realm, is itself a well-worn Celtic pattern.

**Deeper reading:** Monaghan lays out three possibilities, that Arthur began as a god, as a culture hero, or as a real king, and it is tempting to treat them as three rival answers, only one of which can be right. But that may be our problem, not the tradition's. The people who told these stories did not keep a tidy distinction between a god, a folk hero, and a remembered king. A ruler good enough to keep the land fertile was already edging toward the divine. A hero who guarded his people's whole way of life was already more than a man. The three options blur into each other because, in Celtic thinking, they were perhpas never fully that different to each other.

## 5. Why are mountains and landmarks across Britain named after King Arthur?

Because in the popular imagination Arthur swelled into a giant of a figure, and people read his presence straight into the land. Hills, peaks and rocks took his name, marking him out as a hero on a more than human scale.

Gerald of Wales, writing in the late 1100s, describes a mountain ridge that ends in "Cadair Arthur, or Arthur's Chair, so called from two peaks which rise up in the form of a throne. This summit is a very lofty spot and most difficult of access, so that in the minds of simple folk it is thought to have belonged to Arthur, the greatest and most distinguished King of the Britons." (12) Notice who is doing the believing in Gerald's account: not the learned writers, but ordinary local people.

Gerald catches that spoken tradition running alongside the written one, and sometimes flatly contradicting it. The Welsh told him that Gildas attacked his own people so fiercely because Arthur had killed his brother. Yet, Gerald admits, "you will find no book which gives an authentic account of that great prince." (13) The stories lived in people's minds and on their tongues, not on the page. Where the books fell silent, the spoken tradition filled the gap with reasons of its own. Proinsias Mac Cana sums up where this leaves us: "The original Arthur may well have been a historical person, but the King Arthur of medieval romance and his knightly entourage are much larger than life and share many of the mythological traits of the Irish hero Fionn mac Cumhaill and his fiana." (14)

## 6. How did the legend of King Arthur spread from Wales across Europe?

Arthur's story did not stay in Wales. It crossed the sea to Brittany, and from there it carried into France and England, picking up a wider and wider audience and, with it, the haunting belief that this king was not truly dead.

The scholar Antone Minard points to Brittany as the crossing point: "Brittany was probably the conduit by which the Celtic tradition concerning Arthur found its way to a wider audience in France and England. Most of the evidence for this is inference and the sources claimed by later writers — for instance, Marie de France's claim to have translated Breton lais." (15) Exactly how the story crossed over is still argued about. But the Bretons held on to something the Welsh and Cornish held too: the belief that Arthur would come back. Alwyn and Brinley Rees say: "In Wales, Cornwall, and Brittany, at this period, there was a firm popular belief that Arthur was not dead but would return to deliver his people from their enemies." (16) That sleeping-and-returning king is itself an old idea about kingship: the true ruler is not gone, only kept waiting in the Otherworld until his people need him again.

Then, around 1139, Geoffrey of Monmouth wrote his *Historia Regum Britanniae* (the "History of the Kings of Britain"). He took this local Welsh-and-Breton tradition and blew it up into the grand story of Britain's whole legendary past. That book set off what the French called the *matière de Bretagne*, the "matter of Britain": a flood of romances that pulled Celtic material into French and then European storytelling. Through all of it, the old mythical heart of Arthur survived, even as layer after layer of new material piled on top.

Why does Arthur last? Patricia Monaghan suggests "the power of his story is mythical rather than merely historical." (17) The Celtic groundwork is real, and you can still make it out under all the later building. Arthur may have started as a god, as a culture hero, or as a war-leader in the wreckage of Roman Britain. We cannot be sure. But the figure who grew up in Welsh tradition and travelled out across the Celtic world and into European books is, by any honest measure, a figure of Celtic myth.

**Deeper reading:** Put the pieces together and the promised return stops looking like a hopeful afterthought. From the start, this is a man who keeps slipping into the Otherworld and coming back: locked in enchanted prisons and freed, carried off wounded to the island of Afallach. He was always half in that other world. So when the Welsh, the Cornish and the Bretons said he would come again, they were not inventing a new ending out of grief. They were finishing the thought the tradition had been making all along: a king who lived between two worlds was never going to stay put in just one of them, least of all the grave.
