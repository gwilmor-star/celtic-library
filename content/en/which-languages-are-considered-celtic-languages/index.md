Celtic languages constitute a branch of the Indo-European language family, though sources do not confirm the specific Indo-European classification details, traditionally divided into two main groups: Goidelic (Gaelic) languages including Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx; and Brittonic languages including Welsh, Cornish, and Breton.

## Key Facts

- The six surviving Celtic languages are Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx (Goidelic branch) and Welsh, Cornish, Breton (Brittonic branch).
- Gaelic, Irish, and Manx vary from each other about as much as Norse, Swedish, and Danish.
- Welsh and Breton differ from the Gaelic languages about as much as German and Dutch differ from Scandinavian languages.
- The earliest written records of Celtic languages appear in ogam inscriptions dating to the 4th-6th centuries AD.
- Celtic languages developed from an analysis requiring knowledge of Latin, as evidenced by the ogam alphabet's invention.

## Q&A Sections

### What are the main branches of Celtic languages?
Celtic languages are divided into two primary branches: Goidelic (also called Gaelic) and Brittonic. The Goidelic branch includes Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx, while the Brittonic branch comprises Welsh, Cornish, and Breton. These represent the surviving Celtic languages, though historically there were others that have since become extinct.

### How closely related are the different Celtic languages?
The relationship varies significantly between the two branches. Within the Goidelic group, Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx are quite closely related, varying from each other about as much as Norse, Swedish, and Danish do. However, the difference between the Goidelic and Brittonic branches is much greater, with Welsh and Breton varying from the Gaelic languages about as much as German and Dutch differ from the Scandinavian languages.

### What is the earliest evidence for written Celtic languages?
The oldest surviving records of Celtic languages appear in ogam inscriptions carved in stone, with something under four hundred inscriptions surviving. These inscriptions generally consist of personal names in the genitive case, often accompanied by the name of the person's father or ancestor. The earliest inscriptions probably date to the fifth and sixth centuries AD, with some possibly belonging to the fourth century.

### How do regional dialects affect mutual intelligibility?
Despite regional variations, speakers of the same Celtic language can generally understand each other across different areas. A Gaelic speaker can converse freely throughout the Scottish Highlands, and Irish speakers from Connaught can communicate in Islay after a short time, though their accents may betray their origins. However, the dialectal differences can be significant enough that speakers from some Irish districts cannot easily converse with Scottish Gaelic speakers.

## In-Depth Overview

### Historical Development and Classification
Celtic languages belong to the Indo-European language family and represent some of the oldest documented languages of western Europe. The invention of the ogam alphabet demonstrates that analysis of Celtic languages was taking place by at least the fourth century AD, requiring knowledge of Latin for its creation. This early linguistic analysis suggests a sophisticated understanding of the language's structure even in ancient times.

### The Goidelic Branch
The Goidelic or Gaelic languages form one of the two main Celtic branches, comprising Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx. These languages maintain relatively close relationships, with speakers able to communicate across linguistic boundaries with varying degrees of success. Irish and Scottish Gaelic are particularly close, while Manx presents greater challenges for mutual comprehension.

### The Brittonic Branch
Welsh and Breton represent the surviving Brittonic languages, along with the revived Cornish. The Brittonic branch shows greater divergence from Goidelic than exists within either branch individually. Despite this greater separation, speakers familiar with one Celtic language can often recognize words and elements in other Celtic languages when spoken separately.

### Literary and Cultural Context
Celtic languages developed within an aristocratic social system, where professional literary classes composed works for aristocratic patrons. This tradition continued through medieval times until the breakdown of these social systems - in Ireland during the seventeenth century, in Scotland during the eighteenth century, and in Wales, with reservations, finally in the sixteenth century. The oral tradition played a crucial role, with written texts often representing centuries of prior oral transmission.

### Linguistic Documentation
The study of Celtic languages has advanced significantly through modern scholarship, though earlier documentation faced challenges. Many ancient texts survived through oral tradition before being written down, making precise dating difficult based solely on linguistic criteria. The relationship between spoken and written forms remained complex, with the written tradition often serving merely to preserve what primarily existed as spoken literature.
