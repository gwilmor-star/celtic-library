Pwyll Pendefig Dyfed (Pwyll Prince of Dyfed) and Arawn, king of Annwfn (the Celtic Otherworld), are the central figures in a remarkable tale of friendship, shape-shifting, and honour from the First Branch of the Mabinogi. Their story involves a year-long exchange of identities that tests loyalty, establishes lasting friendship, and earns Pwyll the title "Pen Annwfn" (Head of Annwfn).

## Key Facts

- Pwyll encounters Arawn during a hunt in Glyn Cuch, where a territorial dispute over a stag leads to their initial meeting.
- The two kings exchange forms for a year, with Pwyll ruling Annwfn while Arawn governs Dyfed in Pwyll's place.
- Pwyll proves his honour by sleeping chastely beside Arawn's wife throughout the year, despite her beauty.
- At year's end, Pwyll defeats Arawn's enemy Hafgan in single combat, uniting the two realms of Annwfn.
- The successful exchange establishes a lasting friendship, and Pwyll becomes known as "Pwyll Head of Annwfn" ever after.

## Q&A Sections

### How do Pwyll and Arawn first meet?

Their encounter begins with a hunting dispute in Glyn Cuch. Pwyll allows his own hounds to feed on a stag killed by Arawn's supernatural pack, an act that insults the Otherworld king's honour. When Pwyll discovers that his opponent is a crowned king, he immediately shows proper respect and asks how he might redeem their friendship. Arawn proposes a solution that will resolve both their conflict and his ongoing war with another Otherworld king named Hafgan.

### What is the nature of their year-long exchange?

Arawn offers to place Pwyll in his position in Annwfn, giving him his own appearance and the companionship of "the most beautiful woman you have ever seen". In return, Arawn will take Pwyll's form and rule Dyfed. The purpose is for Pwyll to face Hafgan in single combat at the year's end, delivering only one fatal blow to defeat him permanently. Each king must maintain the other's identity so perfectly that no one suspects the deception.

### How does Pwyll prove his honour during the exchange?

Throughout the year in Annwfn, Pwyll demonstrates remarkable restraint and loyalty. Each night when he retires with Arawn's wife, he chastely turns his back to her, refusing any intimate contact. This behaviour contrasts with many similar tales where shape-shifting is used to deceive a spouse. When Arawn returns and resumes relations with his wife, she questions the change in his behaviour, having grown accustomed to a year of chaste companionship.

### What happens when they meet again after the year?

The two kings reunite at the original meeting place in Glyn Cuch, where each expresses gratitude for the other's service. Arawn learns from his wife about Pwyll's faithful conduct, while Pwyll discovers that his own realm of Dyfed was ruled even better than before during Arawn's stewardship. The successful completion of their agreement establishes a permanent friendship between the two kingdoms, and Pwyll's title changes from "Prince of Dyfed" to "Head of Annwfn".

## In-Depth Overview

### The Otherworld Setting and Annwfn

Annwfn represents the Celtic Otherworld, derived from Welsh an- (either an intensifying prefix giving 'very deep/the abyss,' or a privative prefix giving 'not-world') combined with dwfn ('deep' or 'world'). In this tale, rather than being a distant island or underground realm, Annwfn appears as a territory within or adjacent to Pwyll's realm of Dyfed. Arawn rules this supernatural kingdom with all the trappings of earthly monarchy—a court, retinue, and wife. The Otherworld court displays remarkable splendour, with "the most beautifully adorned buildings that anyone had seen" and attendants who serve with perfect protocol.

### The Combat with Hafgan

The central task requires Pwyll to face Hafgan, another king from Annwfn who perpetually wars against Arawn. The combat follows specific supernatural rules: Pwyll must deliver exactly one blow, which will prove fatal, but he must resist any pleas from Hafgan for a second strike. This restriction reflects the otherworldly nature of the conflict, where additional blows would only restore Hafgan's strength. The single combat serves as what scholars identify as fír fer ("men's truth"), an ordeal by battle that reflects belief in the power of truth and righteousness.

### Themes of Hospitality and Exchange

The tale exemplifies the Celtic tradition of reciprocal hospitality and gift exchange between rulers. Both kings prove themselves worthy of friendship through their actions during the exchange period. Pwyll's chaste behaviour toward Arawn's wife demonstrates the highest form of loyalty between sworn friends, while Arawn's excellent governance of Dyfed shows his respect for the alliance. This mutual testing and proving creates a bond stronger than mere political alliance.

### Literary and Mythological Context

The story belongs to the First Branch of the Mabinogi, which scholars recognise as containing some of the oldest mythological material in Welsh literature. The tale bears resemblance to international folk motifs, particularly "The Two Brothers" story type, which often involves chaste sleeping arrangements between friends. However, the Welsh version emphasises honour and friendship rather than romantic deception, setting it apart from similar tales in other traditions.

> 'From Annwfn,' he replied. 'I am Arawn, king of Annwfn.' 'Lord,' said Pwyll, 'how shall I win your friendship?' 'This is how,' he replied. 'A man whose territory is next to mine is forever fighting me. He is Hafgan, a king from Annwfn. By ridding me of that oppression––and you can do that easily––you will win my friendship.'
