The story of Pwyll and Arawn is a foundational tale from the First Branch of the Mabinogi, recounting how Pwyll, Prince of Dyfed, exchanged places with Arawn, king of Annwn (the Celtic Otherworld), for one year. This exchange, born from Pwyll's offense of feeding his hounds on Arawn's stag, tests both rulers and establishes an enduring friendship built on honor and loyalty.

## Key Facts

- Pwyll offended Arawn by allowing his hunting dogs to feed on a stag killed by Arawn's pack, requiring compensation to restore the king's honor.
- The two rulers exchanged forms and kingdoms for exactly one year, with Arawn taking Pwyll's shape to rule Dyfed while Pwyll assumed Arawn's form to rule Annwn.
- Pwyll proved his loyalty by sleeping chastely beside Arawn's wife each night, turning his back to her and refusing intimacy.
- At the year's end, Pwyll defeated Arawn's enemy Hafgan in single combat with one fatal blow, uniting Hafgan's realm with Arawn's.
- After their successful exchange, Pwyll became known as "Pwyll Head of Annwn" rather than "Pwyll Prince of Dyfed".

## Q&A Sections

### How did Pwyll first encounter Arawn?

Pwyll was hunting in Glyn Cuch when he became separated from his companions. He heard the cry of another pack of hounds different from his own and saw a stag being brought down by these otherworldly dogs. When Pwyll's hounds began feeding on the stag, he encountered Arawn, who initially refused to greet him due to his discourtesy. Only after learning that Arawn was a crowned king did Pwyll respond appropriately with "good day to you".

### What was the nature of their exchange agreement?

Arawn proposed that Pwyll take his place in Annwn for one year to defeat his enemy Hafgan, a rival king who constantly fought against him. In return, Arawn would rule Dyfed in Pwyll's form, ensuring that "no man or woman in your realm realizes that I am not you". Arawn would give Pwyll "the most beautiful woman you have ever seen to sleep with you every night, and give you my face and form so that no chamberlain nor officer nor any other person who has ever served me shall know that you are not me".

### How did Pwyll prove his loyalty during the year?

Throughout the year in Annwn, Pwyll demonstrated remarkable fidelity by sleeping chastely beside Arawn's wife, turning his back to her each night despite her beauty. This restraint proved crucial to earning Arawn's trust and gratitude. When Arawn returned to his wife after the year ended, she was surprised by his sudden affection, having grown accustomed to a year of chaste behavior. Arawn learned from his wife how faithfully Pwyll had behaved in his absence.

### What happened when Pwyll faced Hafgan?

At the appointed time, exactly one year after their agreement, Pwyll met Hafgan at the ford while in Arawn's form. Following Arawn's specific instructions, Pwyll struck Hafgan only once, delivering a fatal blow. Despite Hafgan's pleas for another blow, Pwyll refused, as Arawn had warned that additional strikes would only restore Hafgan's strength for future battles. This single decisive blow united Hafgan's realm with Arawn's kingdom.

### What was the outcome of their friendship?

When both rulers returned to their proper forms and kingdoms, they discovered that their realms had prospered under the other's rule. Pwyll found that Dyfed "was ruled better than ever before when Arawn was there in Pwyll's guise". From that time forward, their friendship only increased, with Arawn and Pwyll exchanging gifts of "horses and hunting-dogs and hawks" as tokens of their bond. Pwyll's title changed permanently from "Prince of Dyfed" to "Head of Annwn," commemorating his successful otherworldly adventure.

## In-Depth Overview

### The Hunting Encounter and Initial Conflict

The tale begins with Pwyll hunting in Glyn Cuch, one of his territories in Dyfed, when he becomes separated from his companions. The narrative establishes the supernatural nature of the encounter through Pwyll's observation of otherworldly hounds with their distinctive cry, different from his own pack. When Arawn's supernatural hunting dogs bring down a stag, Pwyll commits a serious breach of protocol by allowing his own hounds to feed on the kill. This action constitutes an insult to Arawn's honor, as the Davies translation notes: "Pwyll has insulted Arawn by feeding his own dogs on the stag killed by Arawn's pack. He must therefore pay compensation to the king for injuring his honour".

The encounter reveals important aspects of medieval Welsh social hierarchy and courtesy. Arawn initially refuses to greet Pwyll properly, demonstrating the gravity of the offense. However, when Pwyll discovers that his adversary is "a crowned king in the land that I come from," he immediately shifts to appropriate royal protocol, asking "how shall I win your friendship?". This exchange introduces Arawn as "king of Annwn," establishing the otherworldly nature of the encounter.

### The Supernatural Exchange Agreement

Arawn's proposal represents one of the most detailed shape-shifting agreements in Celtic literature. The king of Annwn offers to place Pwyll "in my place in Annwfn, and give you the most beautiful woman you have ever seen to sleep with you every night, and give you my face and form so that no chamberlain nor officer nor any other person who has ever served me shall know that you are not me". The completeness of this transformation encompasses physical appearance, royal authority, and intimate relationships, yet includes a crucial test of character through the arrangement with Arawn's wife.

The narrative explains Annwn as "a name for the Celtic Otherworld, derived from an ('in, inside') + dwfn ('world')". Interestingly, rather than being located in a distant realm, Annwn appears to exist "as a land within Pwyll's realm of Dyfed," suggesting the permeable nature of otherworldly boundaries in Welsh tradition. Arawn escorts Pwyll to his court, describing magnificent buildings "the most beautifully adorned buildings that anyone had seen," with halls, sleeping quarters, and chambers.

### The Test of Fidelity and Honor

The central test of Pwyll's character occurs nightly throughout the year-long exchange. Despite being given access to "the most beautiful woman that anyone had seen, wearing a golden garment of shining brocaded silk," Pwyll consistently "chastely turns his back to her" each night. This restraint represents more than mere courtesy; it demonstrates Pwyll's understanding of the deeper bonds of loyalty and friendship that transcend physical temptation.

The Davies translation notes that this episode "bears a resemblance to the folk-tale of 'The Two Brothers', which involves a chaste sleeping together, sometimes with a sword of chastity, as in the Old French Ami et Amile". However, the First Branch emphasizes that "no intercourse takes place" and focuses on "fidelity and loyalty" rather than sexual conquest. This distinguishes the Welsh tale from other shape-shifting stories where the transformation serves primarily sexual purposes.

### The Combat with Hafgan

The climactic confrontation with Hafgan represents the fulfillment of Pwyll's obligation to Arawn. Hafgan is described as "a king from Annwfn" whose territory borders Arawn's realm and who is "forever fighting me". The specific nature of the combat reflects elements of ritual single combat, with Arawn's precise instructions that Pwyll "must give him only one blow—he will not survive it" but must refuse any additional strikes regardless of Hafgan's pleas.

Thomas Charles-Edwards, as noted by Ó Cathasaigh, has demonstrated that "Pwyll's duel with Hafgan is to be compared with the Ordeal by Battle described in Irish texts and known in Irish as fír fer (lit., 'men's truth')". This connection links the combat to concepts of divine judgment and the power of truth in determining right from wrong. The single decisive blow that unites "Hafgan's realm with Arawn's" represents not merely military victory but a restoration of proper order.

### The Transformation of Kingship

The successful completion of their exchange transforms both rulers and their relationship. When they meet again "at Glyn Cuch" after the year's end, each expresses gratitude for the other's service. Arawn learns from his wife "how Pwyll had been faithful to him," while "Pwyll in turn discovers that his land of Dyfed was ruled better than ever before when Arawn was there in Pwyll's guise". This mutual improvement suggests that their exchange benefited both realms through the complementary skills and perspectives each ruler brought.

The permanent change in Pwyll's title from "Prince of Dyfed" to "Head of Annwn" represents more than honorary recognition. The narrative states that "the name Pwyll Prince of Dyfed falls into disuse, and he is called Pwyll Head of Annwn ever after". This transformation connects Pwyll permanently to the otherworldly realm while maintaining his earthly kingdom, creating a unique dual authority that bridges mortal and supernatural worlds.

The ongoing friendship between the two rulers continues through gift exchange, with sources noting that "Pwyll (Pryderi's father) and Arawn, king of the Otherworld, send each other 'horses and hunting-dogs and hawks' as a token of their friendship". These exchanges maintain the bond established through their year-long test and create lasting ties between their realms that extend into subsequent generations of the Mabinogi cycle.
