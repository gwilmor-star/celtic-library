The Celtic nations are six regions recognized for their shared Celtic heritage: Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany (in France), Cornwall (in England), and the Isle of Man. These territories are distinguished by their Celtic languages, cultural traditions, and historical connections to ancient Celtic peoples who once inhabited much of Europe.

## Key Facts

- The six Celtic nations are Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Cornwall, the Isle of Man, and Brittany.
- These regions are defined by their preservation of Celtic languages and cultural traditions despite centuries of external political control.
- Cornwall gained recognition as a Celtic nation in the early 20th century through the efforts of scholars like Henry Jenner.
- Brittany maintains its Celtic identity within France, having been celebrated as "a repository of Celtic culture" since the late 18th century.

## Q&A Sections

### What defines a Celtic nation?
Celtic nations are regions that maintain Celtic languages, cultural practices, and historical connections to the ancient Celtic peoples. These territories share common linguistic roots in the Celtic language family and preserve traditions in music, folklore, and social customs that distinguish them from their surrounding dominant cultures. Recognition as a Celtic nation involves both scholarly acknowledgment of Celtic heritage and active cultural maintenance.

### How did Cornwall gain recognition as a Celtic nation?
Cornwall's status as a Celtic nation was established through early 20th-century revival efforts. "In the first few years of the twentieth century a small band of Cornish revivalists struggled to convince sceptical Celticists in Ireland, Wales, Scotland and Brittany that Cornwall too was Celtic". By 1904, Jenner published "Cornwall: A Celtic Nation" in The Celtic Review, arguing for Cornwall's inclusion among Celtic territories. The Kowethas Kelto-Kernewek, founded in 1901, provided institutional support for this recognition.

### What makes Brittany unique among the Celtic nations?
Brittany stands out as the only Celtic nation located within continental Europe, maintaining its Celtic identity within France. Since Jacques Cambry's 1799 travelogue, the region has been recognized as preserving Celtic culture and traditional practices lost elsewhere in France. Brittany's Celtic heritage includes both linguistic traditions (Breton language) and cultural practices that distinguish it from metropolitan French culture.

## In-Depth Overview

### Historical Recognition and Definition
The concept of Celtic nations emerged from scholarly recognition of shared linguistic and cultural heritage among regions that preserved Celtic traditions. The six recognized Celtic nations—Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Brittany, Cornwall, and the Isle of Man—represent territories where Celtic languages and cultural practices survived the expansion of other European powers. This classification reflects both historical continuity and modern cultural preservation efforts.

### Language and Cultural Preservation
Celtic languages form the primary basis for defining these nations, with each territory maintaining distinct linguistic traditions within the broader Celtic language family. Ireland preserves Irish Gaelic, Scotland maintains Scottish Gaelic, Wales continues Welsh, Brittany speaks Breton, Cornwall has revived Cornish, and the Isle of Man preserves Manx (sources limited). These languages serve as vehicles for transmitting traditional folklore, mythology, and cultural practices that distinguish Celtic nations from their surrounding political entities.

### Scholarly Development and Revival Movements
The formal recognition of Celtic nations developed through scholarly and cultural revival movements beginning in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Cornwall's inclusion required particular advocacy, with Henry Jenner leading efforts to establish Cornwall's Celtic credentials through organizations like the Kowethas Kelto-Kernewek, founded in 1901. Jenner published "Cornwall: A Celtic Nation" in The Celtic Review in 1904, marking formal recognition of Cornwall's status.

### Contemporary Cultural Expression
Modern Celtic nations maintain their identities through participation in pan-Celtic cultural events, festivals, and shared artistic traditions. Cornwall exemplifies this approach through its promotion of "Celtic" music, imagery, and artifacts that connect the region to broader Celtic cultural networks. These contemporary expressions help sustain Celtic identity in the face of globalization and political integration with non-Celtic states.

### Political and Administrative Context
The Celtic nations exist within various political frameworks, from independent states like Ireland to regions within larger nation-states. Brittany maintains its Celtic identity within France, having been recognized since the 18th century as preserving cultural traditions distinct from metropolitan French culture. Scotland and Wales operate as constituent countries within the United Kingdom, while Cornwall functions as a county within England, and the Isle of Man enjoys special status as a British Crown dependency (sources limited).
