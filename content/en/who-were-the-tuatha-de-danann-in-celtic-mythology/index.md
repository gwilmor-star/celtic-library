The Tuatha Dé Danann were the old gods of Ireland: a race of magical beings famous for their beauty, their skill, and their power over the natural world. The monks who wrote their stories down kept trying to demote them into ordinary humans, clever invaders who simply happened to know a great deal of magic, but the divine version of them kept breaking through. Most of what we know comes from Irish texts written between roughly 900 and 1200, and one of the four great cycles of Irish legend, the Mythological Cycle, is almost entirely their story. It runs from a strange beginning in the islands at the top of the world, through two great battles for Ireland, to a last retreat underground that eventually turned the gods into the fairy folk of Irish tradition, who are said to be there still.

## 1. What does 'Tuatha Dé Danann' mean, and were they gods or humans?

The name most likely means something close to "the peoples of the goddess Danu," though scholars still argue over it. And were they gods or humans? The honest answer is both: the medieval writers treated them as divine and human by turns, and never finally chose.

Danu herself is a mysterious figure. She first appears, as "Donann," in a poem by Eochaid ua Flainn, and the connection appears to run backwards: the goddess looks to have been spun out of the people's name rather than the people being named after her. Attempts to tie her to the Welsh goddess Dôn, the Vedic Danu, or the river Danube don't hold up once you look at the language. James MacKillop puts the doubt plainly:

> "Their very name, people of the goddess Danu/Ana, may have been invented in the Lebor Gabála, but the phrase Tuatha Dé was earlier used to describe the old gods or to denote the Israelites in translations of the Bible. The complete origin of the name and the precise implications of it are still disputed." (1)

The beings themselves wouldn't sit still in any one box. To the churchmen they were gods one minute, demons the next, then clever magicians, then unusually gifted humans, whatever the tale required. Knott and Murphy catch the to-and-fro:

> "When in poetic and storytelling mood even the learned made little or no attempt to conceal the fact that the Túatha Dé Donann were a spirit folk. When in historical mood, however, they euhemerized them into a human race, skilled in magic, who once occupied Ireland but were defeated by the invading Goidil or Gaels." (2)

That verb, "euhemerized," simply means recasting a god as a human being of history, and it is a move these writers made again and again. The wobble went deeper still. Even inside the group, the tradition split them in two. A collection of lore put together between 900 and 1100 said that: "among these divinities, their 'people of power' (áes cumhachta) were considered gods (this would likely refer to sages, magicians, warriors, leaders and divine craftspeople) while their herders and farmers (áes trebtha) were non-gods." (3) So even by their own people's reckoning, the Tuatha Dé were not all gods.

**Deeper reading:** Look closely at that last split and something surprising turns up. The line between god and not-god didn't run around the Tuatha Dé as a people; it ran right through the middle of it. Sages, smiths, and warriors counted as gods, while the herders and ploughmen did not. To the people telling these stories, being divine was less a separate kind of being than a high rank in the hall, a place you earned by what your hands and head could do.

Another possibility is that the churchmen didn't quite understand the nature of the beings they were trying do describe. If we look at some of the Irish stories closely, we find that there may not have been such a clean divide between mortal and immortal. We may need to revise our assumption of what 'god' and 'goddess' mean if we're to trully understand who the Tuatha Dé were.

## 2. Where did the Tuatha Dé Danann come from?

They came from somewhere no map can show: four cities in the islands at the far edge of the northern world. They were not born powerful: they trained for it, mastering magic and hidden knowledge before they ever set foot in Ireland.

The oldest surviving account is in the ninth or tenth century, Cath Maige Tuired, "The Second Battle of Mag Tuired":

> "The Túatha Dé Danann were in the northern islands of the world, studying occult lore and sorcery, druidic arts and witchcraft and magical skill, until they surpassed the sages of the pagan arts. They studied occult lore and secret knowledge and diabolic arts in four cities: Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias." (4)

A second tradition, kept in a later retelling, puts these cities in the northern islands of Greece, where the Tuatha Dé learned "druidry, knowledge, prophecy and magic." (5) The exact geography slides around from one text to the next, but what lies underneath it doesn't move: by the time they reached Ireland, the Tuatha Dé already carried a learning older and deeper than anything the island could offer.

According to Cath Maige Tuired, they burned their own ships the moment they landed, so that turning back was no longer possible, and they came wrapped in a mist that hid them for three days. This was not the cautious arrival of settlers looking for somewhere to live, but the entrance of a power that meant to take what it wanted.

## 3. What were the Four Treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann?

The Four Treasures were four objects of power, one carried out of each of the four cities: a stone, a spear, a sword, and a cauldron. Between them they cover the four things a people needs to take a land and hold it: rightful kingship, victory in war, deadly force, and plenty.

The fullest list comes down to us in the Second Battle of Moytura:

> "Out of Falias was brought the Stone of Fál, which was in Tara. It used to roar under every king that would take the realm of Ireland. Out of Gorias was brought the Spear that Lugh had. No battle was ever won against it or him who held it in his hand. Out of Findias was brought the Sword of Nuada. When it was drawn from its deadly sheath, no one ever escaped from it, and it was irresistible. Out of Murias was brought the Dagdae's Caldron. No company ever went from it unthankful." (6)

Professor Jaan Puhvel doesn't read the spear and sword as mere story-props. He says the "first two were talismanic weapons that ensured" military supremacy, and sets them beside the magical objects that turn up again and again right across the Indo-European world. (7) Each treasure even came with its own teacher from the city it left behind, a reminder that the Tuatha Dé didn't get their power for free, but studied for it.

**Deeper reading:** Don't hurry past the small detail that each treasure arrived with its own teacher. The Tuatha Dé couldn't just pick up the Sword of Nuada and swing it; someone from Findias had to teach them. Set that beside where they came from, four cities where they did nothing but study, and a quiet rule of their world shows through. Raw power, on its own, counted for little until someone had learned how to use it. Knowing a thing and owning it were two halves of the same coin.

## 4. Who did the Tuatha Dé Danann fight to win Ireland?

To win Ireland the Tuatha Dé had to fight for it, in two wars against two very different enemies. First came the Fir Bolg, the people already living on the island; then came the Fomoire, a stranger and far more frightening kind of foe.

The war with the Fir Bolg is told simply in Cath Maige Tuired:

> "The Túatha Dé came with a great fleet to Ireland to take it by force from the Fir Bolg. Upon reaching the territory of Corcu Belgatan (which is Conmaicne Mara today), they at once burned their boats so that they would not think of fleeing to them... The battle of Mag Tuired was fought between them and the Fir Bolg. The Fir Bolg were defeated, and 100,000 of them were killed including the king, Eochaid mac Eirc. Núadu's hand was cut off in that battle." (8)

That last detail, Núadu losing his hand, matters a great deal. An Irish king had to be sound in body according to custom; a maimed man couldn't keep the throne (although historically plenty probably did). So the wound forces Núadu out, sets off a scramble over who rules next, and swings the door open to the second and bigger war, against the Fomoire.

The Fomoire were a different order of enemy. Carl Lindahl called them "sinister" beings with "strong aquatic connections," who "oppress" the Tuatha Dé "in a variety of ways." (9) Daragh Smyth points to a long line of readers who see this war as something larger than a fight over land, a clash between two halves of the world: "The Tuatha Dé Danann were lords of life and light, and the Fomorians lords of darkness and death." (10) When the Second Battle came, the great champion of the Tuatha Dé was Lug. He broke past the guard set over him and threw himself to the front:

> "it was he who was in front of the battalion of the Túatha Dé. Then a keen and cruel battle was fought between the race of the Fomoire and the men of Ireland. Lug was urging the men of Ireland to fight the battle fiercely so they should not be in bondage any longer." (11)

## 5. Who were the main gods of the Tuatha Dé Danann?

The Tuatha Dé weren't a faceless crowd. They were a whole cast of named gods, the Dagda and Lug among the greatest, each one famous for a particular gift or craft.

The Annals of Inisfallen run through the main names like a clerk taking attendance:

> "the Dagda, Mac ind Óc, Lug son of Eithliu, Dian Cécht, Goibnenn the smith, Luchtaine the wright, and Crédne the craftsman." (12)

Dian Cécht is the healer, Goibnenn the smith, Luchtaine the carpenter, alongside the others they were a workshop's worth of skilled hands. The pantheon laid out the way a working society divides up its trades.

Several of these gods reach far beyond Ireland. Knott and Murphy point out that Lug matches a god worshipped right across the Continent, the one "who gave his name to Lyons (Lugudunum), Laon, Leyden, and other continental towns," while Núadu matches "Nodons (or Nodens) who was worshipped in a Romano-British temple at Lydney Park in Gloucestershire." (13) Brigit lines up with the goddess Brigantia. Even the Dagda's name hints at what he does.

The Dagda is worth pausing on. MacLeod sums up what the old sources keep:

> "One of the most important Irish deities was known as the Dagda, 'The Good God,' as in 'good at many things'... He was a king or chief of the Tuatha Dé Danann, associated with abundance and fertility, the weather and the harvest, druidic magic and protection, and the powers of life and death. He owned an inexhaustible cauldron of nourishment, a club that wielded life from one end and death from the other, and a magic harp." (14)

In that list you are looking at the whole group gathered into one figure: weather, plenty, the crafts, and the power over life and death all held in the same two hands.

**Deeper reading:** There's a logic to who stands at the top. The Irish asked of a king exactly what the Dagda is: not the finest fighter or the best farmer in the land, but the one man answerable for fighting and farming and weather and justice together. The lesser gods can each keep to a single craft. The chief, like a king, has to carry the lot, because someone must stand for the whole. Lug played a similar role in The Second Battle of Moytura.

## 6. What happened to the Tuatha Dé Danann after they were defeated?

After a new people, the Milesians, beat them in battle, the Tuatha Dé didn't die out and they didn't sail away. They went underground, into the green mounds and ancient tombs of Ireland, and over time they became the fairy folk who, in Irish tradition, are there still.

The Milesians are the Gaels, the people the Irish saw as their own ancestors. When the fighting was done, the two sides carved up the world between them, as Monaghan describes:

> "The Milesians took the surface world, while the Tuatha Dé took the rest: the misty Islands out to sea, the bottoms of Lakes, and the Fairy Mounds." (15)

Graham says:

> "After their defeat by the Milesians, the Tuatha Dé retreated forever into the sídhe — the mounds, passage-tombs and fairy-forts that dot the Irish landscape." (16)

The great Stone Age tombs of the Boyne Valley, Newgrange chief among them, became in these stories the halls and palaces of the beaten gods. Life under the hills was no sad afterlife, either. Centuries on, the Fate of the Children of Lir still finds them down there, "consuming the Feast of Age, merrily and happily, without fatigue and without uneasiness." (17) They became the aes síde, "the people of the mounds." That word, síde, is the one hiding inside "banshee": a bean sídhe is simply a woman of the mounds. Monaghan points to two festivals, Beltane (1 May) and Samhain (1 November), as the times when "the veils between the worlds lifted" and these old gods and the late-coming humans could meet once more. (15)

**Deeper reading:** Notice where the beaten gods went. Not up to some far-off heaven, not off the western edge of the map, but down: into the very hills the living Irish walked over every day, into tombs already old beyond memory when the tales were told. Losing the war didn't banish the Tuatha Dé from the world so much as move them one floor down in it. And being only a floor down, they stayed near enough to visit, so that on a couple of nights a year the door between the two worlds stood open. For the Irish, the other world was never a far country. It was the underside of the ground beneath their feet.

## 7. Why did medieval Irish writers turn the gods into humans?

Because the men holding the pens were Christian monks, and a Christian could hardly admit that a crowd of pagan gods had once truly ruled Ireland. Their fix was to demote the gods into humans, clever and magical humans, but humans all the same, and to fit them into a grand history of Ireland that began with the Bible.

There's a name for this trick of turning old gods into figures of history: euhemerism. The book that performs it is the Lebor Gabála Érenn, the "Book of the Taking of Ireland," which lays Ireland's past out as a chain of invasions, one people arriving after another. Puhvel notes where the chain begins: "biblically with a certain Cessair, a granddaughter of Noah, and antedating the Flood." (18) Slotted into that line-up, the Tuatha Dé became just one set of incomers among many, marked out not as gods but as people with an alarming gift for magic.

MacKillop describes what was going on:

> "Unquestionably many members of the Tuatha Dé Danann pre-date the composition of the Lebor Gabála, having been the gods of pre-Christian cults. But from the Lebor Gabála onward they are portrayed as humans, if extraordinary ones." (1)

The gods were never deleted from the record, only reclassified.

The two readings, gods and humans, never settled their quarrel. As Knott and Murphy saw, the poets and storytellers kept slipping back into the older habit, treating the Tuatha Dé as spirits the moment a tale needed them to be. The careful historians tidied the gods into history; the poets kept letting them back out. And that tug-of-war, carried on inside the work of the same monks across the same few centuries, may be the truest thing the tradition ever tells us about the Tuatha Dé: they were too mighty to file away cleanly, and too useful to give up.
