The Tuatha Dé Danann (also spelled Tuatha Dé Donann) were the divine race or "tribe of the gods" in Irish mythology, depicted in medieval texts as supernatural beings who ruled Ireland before the arrival of the Milesians.

## Key Facts

- The name Tuatha Dé Danann first appears in the verse of Eochaid ua Flainn (†1004), with earlier sources referring to them as Fir Dé (Men of the gods) or Tuath Dé (Tribes of the gods).
- They learned lore, magic, druidism and wizardry in four northern cities—Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias—bringing four magical treasures to Ireland.
- The Tuatha Dé Danann defeated the Fir Bolg in the first battle of Mag Tuired and later fought the Fomorians in the second battle.
- After their defeat by the Milesians, they retreated into hills and tumuli (síd-mounds) to become the síd-beings or fairy folk of later Irish tradition.
- They were ruled by kings including Nuadu Argatlám and later Bodhbh Dearg, son of the Dagda.

## Q&A Sections

### What does the name "Tuatha Dé Danann" mean?
The name means "Tribes of the goddess Donann," though this figure Donann appears to be a late invention. Koch's encyclopedia explains that the earliest form was simply Tuath Dé (Tribes of the gods), expanded to avoid confusion with the biblical "People of God". The name is grammatically problematic, with Donann behaving like an indeclinable genitive rather than a normal Irish proper noun, suggesting the goddess was created to explain the group name rather than the reverse.

### Where did the Tuatha Dé Danann come from according to the myths?
According to Cath Maige Tuired, "The Tuatha Dé Danann were in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom". They studied in four cities: Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias, each under a wizard-teacher, before coming to Ireland with four magical treasures.

### What happened to them after the Milesian conquest?
After their defeat by the sons of Míl (Milesians), the Tuatha Dé Danann "went into hills and the tumuli [síd-brugaib], so that the síd-beings under the earth granted hostages to them". They became the supernatural inhabitants of the otherworld mounds (síd), leaving five men in each province to create conflicts among the Milesians. In later tradition, they transformed into the fairy folk or síd-people of Irish folklore.

### Who were their most important kings and leaders?
Their early king was Nuadu Argatlám, who ruled for twenty years after losing his arm in the first battle of Mag Tuired and having it replaced with a silver one by the physician Dian Cécht. Later, they chose Bodhbh Dearg, son of the Dagda, as their king after much deliberation among the nobles. Other prominent figures reportedly included the Dagda himself, Lugh, and Manannán mac Lir, though the sources do not confirm all details about their leadership roles.

## In-Depth Overview

### Origins and Mythological Arrival
The Tuatha Dé Danann represent the divine pantheon of pre-Christian Ireland, portrayed in medieval literature as one of the successive waves of settlers who conquered the island. The Second Battle of Mag Tuired describes their mystical origins: they came from four otherworldly cities in the northern isles, where they mastered supernatural arts under four wizard-teachers. Each city contributed a magical treasure: the Stone of Fál from Falias, which would cry out under the rightful king of Ireland; Nuadu's sword from Gorias; Lugh's invincible spear from Findias; and the Dagda's inexhaustible cauldron from Murias.

### Political Structure and Kingship
The Tuatha Dé Danann operated as a complex political confederation rather than a simple monarchy. After the first battle of Mag Tuired, they faced succession disputes when King Nuadu lost his arm and temporary fitness to rule. The text reveals their democratic decision-making process: "the chiefs of the Tuatha Dé Danann said: 'It is better for us to have one king over us, than to be divided as we are, serving various kings throughout Erinn'". Among the candidates for kingship were Bodhbh Dearg, Ilbreach of Eas Ruaidh, Lir of Sidh Fionnachaidh, and Midhir the Proud of Bri Leith.

### Conflicts with Other Mythological Races
The Tuatha Dé Danann's mythology centers on their conflicts with two other supernatural races. They first fought the Fir Bolg in the first battle of Mag Tuired, "in which a hundred thousand of the Fir Bolg fell". More significantly, they engaged in an epic struggle with the Fomorians, a shadowy race associated with chaos and the sea. This conflict represents the Indo-European mythological pattern of wars between different groups of gods, with complex kinship relationships linking individuals from both sides.

### Transformation into the Otherworld
Following their defeat by the Milesians, the Tuatha Dé Danann underwent a transformation that would define Irish supernatural beliefs for centuries. Rather than disappearing, they "went into hills and the tumuli [síd-brugaib]" and established themselves as rulers of the otherworld. This retreat created the foundation for the síd-tradition in Irish folklore, where these former gods became the fairy folk inhabiting burial mounds and otherworldly realms. Their continued presence in the landscape meant they remained active in human affairs, sometimes helping and sometimes hindering mortal endeavors.

## Primary Sources

***Cath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> The Tuatha Dé Danonn were in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom. There were four cities in which they were learning lore and science and diabolic arts, to wit Falias and Gorias, Murias and Findias. Out of Falias was brought the Stone of Fál, which was in Tara. It used to roar under every king that would take the realm of Ireland.

***Cath Maige Tuired (Second Battle of Mag Tuired)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Stokes, Whitley, Revue Celtique 12 (1891)*
> Now the Tuatha Dé made an alliance with the Fomorians, and Balor, grandson of Nét, gave his daughter Ethne to Cian son of Dian-cecht, bind the alliance of the Fomorians to them. For his father, even Elatha son of Delbaeth, was king of the Fomorians.

***The Celtic Heroic Age*** *(c. 12th century)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, Celtic Studies Publications (2003)*
> It is the Tuatha De Donann who brought with them the Great Fil which was in Tara, namely, the stone of knowledge after which Ireland is called Mag Fail. The one beneath whom it shrieked used to be king of Ireland, until Cu Chulainn split it; for it did not shriek beneath him or his foster son, Lugaid son of the Three White Ones of Emain.

***Aided Chloinne Lir (The Fate of the Children of Lir)*** *(c. 11th century)*
*transl. in: O'Curry, Eugene, rev. O'Duffy, Richard J., Gaelic Union (1883)*
> The chiefs of the Tuatha Dé Danann said: 'It is better for us,' said they, 'to have one king over us, than to be divided as we are, serving various kings throughout Erinn.' Among the chiefs of these bodies who expected to obtain sovereignty for themselves, over the Tuatha Dé Danann, were Bodhbh Dearg, son of the Daghdha; and Ilbhreach of Eas-Ruaidh; and Lir of Sidh Fionnachaidh; and Midhir the Proud of Bri Leith.

***Mesca Ulad (The Intoxication of the Ulstermen)*** *(c. 8th century)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, Celtic Studies Publications (2003)*
> The Tuath De Danann went into hills and the tumuli [síd-brugaib], so that the síd-beings under the earth granted hostages to them [as a token of submission]. [The Tuath De] left five men of their people for each province in Ireland making a proliferation of battles, conflicts, strife, and aggression amongst the sons of MU.

***The Celtic Heroic Age*** *(c. 12th century)*
*transl. in: Koch, John T. & Carey, John, Celtic Studies Publications (2003)*
> Nuadu Argatlam, then, was king of the Tuath De Donann for seven years before coming to Ireland, until his arm was cut off in the first battle of Mag Tuired. [...] then Nuadu Argatlam ruled for twenty years. Dian Cecht the physician, helped by Creidne the artisan, put a silver arm on him, with the full vigour of an arm in every finger and in every joint.

***Tochmarc Étaíne (The Wooing of Étaín)*** *(c. 9th century)*
*transl. in: Bergin, Osborn & Best, Richard Irvine, Ériu 12 (1938)*
> After a time Fuamnach came on a visit to Midir, and along with her as sureties came the three gods of Dana, namely Lug and the Dagda, and Ogma. Midir reproached Fuamnach exceedingly and said to her that she should not go from him were it not for the power of the sureties that had brought her.
