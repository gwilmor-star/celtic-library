The Tuatha Dé Danann were a supernatural race of divine beings in Irish mythology, considered the gods of pre-Christian Ireland who conquered the land before being defeated by mortal invaders and retreating to the Otherworld (sources limited).

## Key Facts

- The name "Tuatha Dé Danann" derives from Old Irish meaning "peoples of the goddess Danu".
- They were portrayed as magicians, druids, and powerfully knowledgeable artisans in early medieval Irish literature.
- In the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, they defeated the Fomoiri through the collaboration of their entire society.
- They represent medieval reflections of the deities of pre-Christian Irish religion.
- After their defeat by the Milesians, they withdrew to the Otherworld where they continued to exist as supernatural beings (sources limited).

## Q&A Sections

### What does the name Tuatha Dé Danann mean?
The name derives from Old Irish "Tûatha Dé Danann," which comes from an earlier form meaning "peoples, nations, tribes of the goddess Danu". The term "tuatha" means community or ethnic group, while the sources do not confirm the specific meaning of "Dé" (sources limited). The hypothetical nominative form "Danu" is not found in medieval Irish texts but is a modern scholarly reconstruction based on the genitive "Danann".

### How were they portrayed in medieval Irish literature?
In early medieval Irish literature, the Tuatha Dé Danann were depicted as supernatural beings with magical abilities. They were described as magicians, druids, or powerfully knowledgeable artisans who possessed extraordinary skills. They were traditionally slotted into the pseudohistorical scheme as one of the primeval waves of invaders who shaped the land and institutions of Ireland.

### What was their role in the Battle of Mag Tuired?
In Cath Maige Tuired (The Second Battle of Mag Tuired), the Tuatha Dé Danann defeated the vast invading army of the Fomoiri at Mag Tuired in Sligo. Their victory rested upon the collaboration of an entire society, including smiths, physicians, and other specialists working together. This battle represents a key mythological narrative where the divine beings successfully defended their realm against chaotic forces.

### What happened to them after the Milesian conquest?
After being defeated by the Milesians, the sources do not confirm the specific details of their withdrawal to the Otherworld, though they reportedly continued to exist as supernatural beings in Irish mythology (sources limited).

## In-Depth Overview

### Divine Nature and Religious Significance
The Tuatha Dé Danann represent the medieval Irish understanding of their pre-Christian deities. Henri d'Arbois de Jubainville identified them as part of "the divine world of Ireland," describing them as "the Tribes of the Goddess Donann" or "Tribes of the Gods". They were understood to be the medieval reflections of the deities worshipped by the pre-Christian Irish, maintaining their supernatural character even in Christianized texts.

### Mythological Conflicts and Narratives
The primary mythological narrative involving the Tuatha Dé Danann centers on their conflicts with other supernatural races, particularly the Fomoiri. These conflicts have been interpreted by scholars as representing an Irish version of the Indo-European "War of the Gods" motif, with the Tuatha Dé Danann representing forces of order against the chaotic Fomoiri. Two influential texts, Cath Maige Tuired and Lebor Gabála Érenn, provide the foundation for understanding these mythological conflicts.

### Literary and Cultural Transformation
Over time, the portrayal of the Tuatha Dé Danann evolved within Irish literary tradition. While maintaining their supernatural characteristics, they became integrated into pseudohistorical narratives that presented Ireland's mythological past as a series of invasions and conquests. This transformation reflects the medieval Irish attempt to reconcile pagan mythological traditions with Christian historical frameworks, creating a unique synthesis that preserved ancient religious narratives within acceptable literary forms.
