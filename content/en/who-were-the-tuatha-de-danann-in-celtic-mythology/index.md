The Tuatha Dé Danann were the supernatural race of gods in pre-Christian Irish mythology. They arrived in Ireland from four northern cities bringing magical treasures, reportedly defeated the Fir Bolg at the First Battle of Magh Tuireadh, though the sources do not confirm this exact title (sources limited), but were later conquered by the Milesians and retreated to the Otherworld where they became the síde or fairy folk of Irish tradition.

## Key Facts
- The name means "People of the goddess Danu" and they were considered the gods of ancient Ireland.
- They learned druidry and magic in four northern cities: Falias, Gorias, Murias, and Findias, each possessing a sacred treasure.
- They defeated the Fir Bolg in the First Battle of Magh Tuireadh and took control of Ireland.
- They were later conquered by the Sons of Míl (Milesians) and withdrew to the Otherworld as invisible beings.
- Medieval Irish writers sometimes portrayed them as fallen angels or highly skilled magicians rather than outright gods.

## Q&A Sections
### What does the name "Tuatha Dé Danann" mean?
The name translates as "People of the goddess Danu" or "Tribes of the goddess Danu". According to Cormac's Glossary, Ana or Danann was "the mother of the three chieftains of the Tuatha dé Danann, Brian, Iuchar, and Iucharbu, who were accounted gods for their feats of necromancy". The goddess Dana was later identified with Brigit in middle Irish times, and the Christian St. Brigit inherited much of her cult.

### Where did the Tuatha Dé Danann come from according to Irish tradition?
They originated "in the northern isles of the world, learning lore and magic and druidism and wizardry and cunning, until they surpassed the sages of the arts of heathendom". There were four cities where they acquired their knowledge: "Falias and Gorias, Murias and Findias". Each city was associated with a wizard-teacher and contained one of their four great treasures.

### What were the four treasures of the Tuatha Dé Danann?
From each of their four cities came a magical treasure: "Out of Falias was brought the Stone of Fal, which was in Tara. It used to roar under every king that would take the realm of Ireland. Out of Gorias was brought the Spear that Lug had. No battle was ever won against it or him who held it in his hand. Out of Findias was brought the Sword of Nuada. When it was drawn from its deadly sheath, no one ever escaped from it, and it was irresistible. Out of Murias was brought the Dagda's Cauldron. No company ever went from it unthankful".

### How did they become the fairy folk of Irish tradition?
After being defeated by the Sons of Míl, the sources describe their transformation, though the details are not fully confirmed in available passages (sources limited). They became the síde, dwelling in fairy mounds and hills throughout Ireland. Modern Irish tradition still recognizes them as "the ever-living invisible Fairy-People of modern Ireland".

### What role did they play in the mythological invasions of Ireland?
In the artificial prehistoric schema of medieval Irish historians, the Tuatha Dé Danann were one of the successive waves of invaders who settled Ireland. They "came in dark clouds" and "put darkness on the sun for three days and three nights" upon their arrival. They "demanded battle or the kingship from the Fir Bolg" and defeated them at the First Battle of Magh Tuireadh, killing "a hundred thousand of the Fir Bolg".

## In-Depth Overview
### Origins and Arrival
The Tuatha Dé Danann represent one of the most significant supernatural races in Irish mythology, functioning as the pantheon of pre-Christian Irish gods. According to the medieval compilation Lebor Gabála Érenn (The Book of Invasions), they were "the descendants of Bethach son of Iarbonel the prophet, son of Nemed" who had been "in the northern islands of the world, learning druidry and knowledge and prophecy and cunning till they were skilled in the arts of heathen learning".

Their arrival in Ireland was marked by supernatural phenomena. They "came in dark clouds" and "occupied the mountains of Conmaicne Róin in Connaught, and they put darkness on the sun for three days and three nights". This dramatic entrance established their otherworldly nature and superior magical abilities from the outset.

### The Four Cities and Sacred Treasures
Central to their mythology are the four northern cities where they acquired their supernatural knowledge and treasures. Each city was associated with a master wizard: "Morfesa was in Falias: Esras was in Gorias: Uscias was in Findias: Semias was in Murias. Those are the four poets of whom the Tuatha De learnt lore and science".

From these cities came their four great treasures, each with specific magical properties that would shape Ireland's destiny. The Stone of Fál became the coronation stone at Tara, validating legitimate kings through its supernatural roar. Lugh's spear guaranteed victory in battle, while Nuada's sword was described as irresistible when drawn. The Dagda's cauldron provided inexhaustible hospitality, ensuring no company ever left unsatisfied.

### Political Structure and Leadership
The Tuatha Dé Danann established a complex political system upon taking Ireland. Initially ruled by Nuada, they faced a succession crisis when he "lost one of his arms in battle, and, being blemished, he had to relinquish the kingship". The resulting political maneuvering reveals sophisticated social structures and kinship obligations.

The choice of Bres as successor demonstrates the importance of diplomatic alliances, as his mixed parentage (Túatha Dé Danann mother, Fomorian father) was intended to cement peace between the two supernatural races. However, this arrangement proved disastrous when "the Fomoiri imposed tribute on Ireland" and "the Túatha Dé Danann were put to work for Bres, performing menial tasks".

Later succession arrangements show more democratic elements. In the Fate of the Children of Lir, "the chiefs of the Tuatha Dé Danann said: 'It is better for us,' said they, 'to have one king over us, than to be divided as we are, serving various kings throughout Erinn'". The council chose Bodhbh Dearg "for three reasons, namely: for the sake of his father; for his own sake; and on account" of his qualifications.

### Social Organization and Feasting
The Tuatha Dé Danann maintained elaborate social customs, particularly around feasting and hospitality. They "were at that time consuming the Feast of Age in each Sioth in turn", indicating a rotational system of ceremonial gatherings across their various otherworld dwellings.

These feasts served important social functions, bringing together the scattered community and reinforcing bonds between different síde. When they visited "Sioth Lir, these four were their joy and their delight, for the beauty and symmetry of their form", referring to the children of Lir whose tragic fate became one of Ireland's most famous tales.

### Transformation into the Síde
The most significant transformation in their mythology occurred with the arrival of the Milesians (Sons of Míl), who represented the Gaelic ancestors of historical Ireland. Rather than being completely vanquished, "The Tuatha De Danann then retired before the invaders, without, however, giving up their sacred Island".

This withdrawal marked their evolution from ruling gods to the síde or fairy folk of ongoing Irish tradition. They became the supernatural inhabitants of hills, mounds, and otherworld realms throughout Ireland.

### Relationship with Mortals
Even after their withdrawal from direct rule, the Tuatha Dé Danann maintained complex relationships with mortal Ireland. They continued to interact with humans, as evidenced in later texts like the Acallam na Senórach, where Finn and his warriors encounter various síde figures.

The children of Lir's transformation into swans demonstrates their continued presence in the mortal world. "The Milesian Clanns, too, no less did they come from every point of Erinn that they might take up an encampment at Loch Dairbhreach in like manner; for historians do not count any music or delight that ever was heard in Erinn in comparison to the music of these swans".

### Legacy and Interpretation
Medieval Christian writers struggled with how to categorize these clearly divine figures within their theological framework. Some "explained the Tuath Dé as fallen angels who were neither wholly good nor evil, or ancient people who became highly skilled in magic, but several writers acknowledged that at least some of them had been gods".

Modern scholarship recognizes several Tuatha Dé Danann figures as cognate with continental Celtic deities, with "Lugh with Lugus, Brigit with Brigantia, Nuada with Nodons, Ogma with Ogmios, and Goibniu with Gobannus". This demonstrates their connection to the broader Celtic pantheon across Europe.
