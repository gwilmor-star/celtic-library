Annwfn (also spelled Annwn) is a Welsh word meaning "the Otherworld" or "the underworld," derived from either an intensive prefix "an-" plus "dwfn" (deep), meaning "very deep," or from a privative "an-" plus "dwfn" (world), meaning "not-world".

## Key Facts

- Annwfn appears in Welsh literature as the realm of the dead and the Otherworld, often described as being beneath the earth.
- The word has two possible etymologies: "very deep" (an- + dwfn) or "not-world" (privative an- + dwfn).
- In the First Branch of the Mabinogi, Pwyll reportedly becomes Lord of Annwfn after helping its king Arawn, though the sources do not confirm this precise sequence.
- Annwfn is associated with magical animals, particularly swine that were brought from there to the mortal world.
- The realm appears in the poem "Preideu Annwfn" (The Spoils of Annwfn), though attribution to Taliesin is not confirmed in the provided sources.

## Q&A Sections

### What are the two main etymological interpretations of Annwfn?

The word Annwfn has two possible derivations according to Welsh scholars. The first interpretation suggests it comes from an intensive prefix "an-" combined with "dwfn" meaning "deep," thus translating to "very deep." The second interpretation proposes a privative "an-" prefix with "dwfn" meaning "world," resulting in "not-world". Both etymologies reflect the otherworldly nature of this realm in Welsh mythology.

### How is Annwfn described in medieval Welsh poetry?

In the Book of Taliesin, Annwfn is described as being "beneath the earth/world" and connected with poetic inspiration. The poem "Angar Kyfondawt" specifically states it is "eluyd" (beneath the earth/world), and in another section it has or is associated with flowing water, possibly a "silver stream". The realm appears as a source of supernatural power and wisdom in these poetic traditions.

### What role do the swine of Annwfn play in Welsh literature?

The swine of Annwfn feature prominently in Welsh tales as magical animals brought from the Otherworld. According to the Triads, Pwyll Lord of Annwfn brought seven swine and gave them to his foster-father Penndaran Dyfed, who kept them in Glyn Cuch in Emlyn. These otherworldly swine were so valuable and well-protected that even Arthur, March, Cai, and Bedwyr could not obtain even one piglet "neither by force, nor by deception, nor by stealth".

### What is the connection between Annwfn and Pwyll in the Mabinogi?

Pwyll reportedly gains the title "Lord of Annwfn" through his encounter with Arawn, the king of Annwfn, at Glyn Cuch in Emlyn, though the sources do not confirm this precise sequence of events. This meeting leads to Pwyll's role in the Otherworld and his acquisition of the magical swine from Annwfn. The location of Glyn Cuch, where the river Cuch forms the northern boundary between Pembrokeshire and Carmarthenshire, becomes significant as the site where otherworldly and mortal realms intersect.

## In-Depth Overview

### Etymology and Linguistic Analysis

The etymology of Annwfn reflects the complex nature of this otherworldly concept in Welsh tradition. Scholars have proposed two primary derivations for the word, both of which capture different aspects of its mythological significance. The first interpretation, "very deep" (an- + dwfn), emphasizes the vertical cosmology often found in Celtic otherworld concepts, where the realm of the dead or supernatural beings exists in the depths beneath the mortal world. The second interpretation, "not-world" (privative an- + dwfn), suggests a realm that exists outside or parallel to the normal world of human experience.

### Literary Appearances and Characteristics

Annwfn appears throughout medieval Welsh literature as a consistent otherworld concept. In the legendary poems of the Book of Taliesin, it is explicitly described as being "beneath the earth/world" and serves as a source of poetic inspiration. The poem "Angar Kyfondawt" makes this spatial relationship clear, while other sections associate Annwfn with flowing water or possibly a "silver stream," suggesting both depth and fluidity as characteristics of this realm.

The most famous literary treatment appears in the poem "Preideu Annwfn" (The Spoils of Annwfn), where "the title preideu Annwn added by John Lewis, the owner of the manuscript in the 16-17c". The word "preideu" can mean either "spoils, booty" or "herds," both interpretations fitting the narrative pattern of otherworld raids common in Celtic literature.

### The Swine of Annwfn Tradition

The tradition of magical swine originating from Annwfn represents one of the most detailed examples of otherworld animals in Welsh literature. According to the Triads of the Island of Britain, seven swine were brought from Annwfn, though this specific number may be conventional rather than mythologically significant. Pwyll's role as their guardian after receiving them from Arawn establishes him as "Lord of Annwfn" and demonstrates the ongoing connection between the otherworld and mortal realm.

The protective nature of these animals is emphasized in the tale of Drystan son of Tallwch, where even the combined efforts of Arthur, March, Cai, and Bedwyr prove insufficient to obtain a single piglet. This reflects the broader Celtic pattern of otherworld animals being both highly valuable and magically protected, often serving as catalysts for major conflicts or quests.
