Sídhe (pronounced "shee") is an Old Irish term meaning "fairy mounds" or "otherworld dwellings" that refers both to the supernatural beings of Celtic mythology and the ancient burial mounds they were believed to inhabit. The term derives from proto-Celtic *sīdos meaning "abode" and is related to English words "seat" and "settle".

## Key Facts

- Sídhe originally denoted ancient burial mounds and prehistoric earthworks that served as portals to the Otherworld.
- The term evolved to refer to the supernatural beings themselves, known as the aos sí or "folk of the fairy mounds".
- In Old Irish manuscripts, the Tuatha Dé Danann are described as "hosts of siabra" (fairies, sprites, or ghosts) and characterized as "gods and not-gods," though the sources do not confirm their specific dwelling within the sídhe after defeat by the Milesians (sources limited).
- The earliest documentary reference to the sí dates to the seventh century AD, though it was already archaic by that time.
- Medieval Irish seers distinguished between different orders of sídhe beings, including shining beings and opalescent beings of greater stature.

## Q&A Sections

### What is the difference between sídhe as places and sídhe as beings?
The term sídhe has a dual meaning in Irish tradition. Originally, it referred to the physical mounds and hills, particularly ancient burial sites and prehistoric earthworks, which were considered entrances to the Otherworld. Over time, the word came to denote the supernatural inhabitants of these mounds themselves. This semantic shift reflects the intimate connection between the physical landscape and the spiritual beings believed to dwell within it.

### How do the sídhe relate to the Tuatha Dé Danann?
The sídhe are closely identified with the Tuatha Dé Danann, the divine race of Irish mythology. According to tradition, when the Milesians conquered Ireland, the Tuatha Dé Danann retreated into the sídhe mounds, becoming the aos sí or fairy folk. In medieval manuscripts like the Book of Leinster, the Tuatha Dé Danann are described as "hosts of siabra" (fairies, sprites, or ghosts) and characterized as "gods and not-gods".

### What characteristics do Irish seers attribute to sídhe beings?
Irish mystics and seers describe the sídhe as appearing in two main forms: shining beings of approximately human stature, and opalescent beings of about fourteen feet in height who seem to hold positions as chiefs or princes. They are described as "a race of majestic appearance and marvellous beauty, in form human, yet in nature divine". These beings are said to draw their life "out of the Soul of the World" and possess both male and female forms, as well as forms that suggest no sexual differentiation.

### How do the sídhe interact with mortal humans in Irish tradition?
The sídhe maintain constant intercourse with the human world, according to Irish seers who claim to behold them regularly. Tales describe them as capable of healing mortals, as seen when Cailte receives miraculous healing in a sídhe dwelling. The tradition suggests that humans who think much of the sídhe during life and see them frequently may enter their world after death. The sídhe also engage in warfare among themselves and sometimes accept human warriors as guests or allies.

## In-Depth Overview

### Etymology and Linguistic Development
The word sídhe derives from Old Irish sí (earlier síd), ultimately from proto-Celtic *sīdos meaning "abode" or "seat". This etymology connects it to concepts of dwelling and settlement, reflecting the fundamental association between these supernatural beings and specific geographical locations. The plural form sídhe encompasses both the physical mounds and their otherworldly inhabitants, demonstrating the fluid boundary between place and person in Celtic cosmology.

### Physical Description and Hierarchy
Medieval Irish sources present detailed descriptions of sídhe beings based on visionary experiences. Seers distinguish between different orders: the shining beings, described as being of human stature or slightly taller, and the more rarely seen opalescent beings who appear as great chiefs or princes standing approximately fourteen feet tall. These latter beings "seem lit up by a light within themselves" and are considered higher in the supernatural hierarchy. The sídhe are consistently portrayed as beautiful and majestic, possessing both male and female forms.

### Dwelling Places and Sacred Geography
The sídhe inhabit ancient burial mounds, prehistoric earthworks, and natural features of the landscape. Specific locations like the Sídh of Assaroe, the Sídh of Ilbrecc, and the Sídh of the Hill of Howth are mentioned in medieval literature as dwelling places of named sídhe lords and their retinues. These locations serve as portals between the human world and the Otherworld, where "there is eternal youth and never-ending joy". The tradition of naming hills and mounds after their sídhe inhabitants persisted into modern times, though many such names have been lost or anglicized.

### Social Organization and Warfare
The sídhe possess complex social structures with kings, queens, and hierarchical ranks. Sources describe kings like Ilbrecc of Assaroe and Bodb Derg leading retinues of hundreds of followers. They engage in organized warfare among different sídhe communities, with detailed accounts of battles and territorial conflicts preserved in texts like the Acallam na Senórach. Their society includes specialized roles such as warriors, musicians, and healers, suggesting a sophisticated civilization parallel to human society but possessed of supernatural powers.

### Interaction with Mortal Realm
The relationship between sídhe and humans involves healing, hospitality, and occasional conflict. Tales describe mortals being welcomed into sídhe dwellings, receiving supernatural healing and entertainment. The sídhe possess magical objects and abilities, including healing tubes, supernatural music, and the power to grant enhanced perception to mortals. However, they also engage in raids and territorial disputes that can involve human allies or adversaries.

*cited in: Evans-Wentz, The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries (1911)*
> In the Book of Leinster the poem of Eochaid records that the Tuatha De Danann, the conquerors of the Fir-Bolgs, were hosts of siabra; and siabra is an Old Irish word meaning fairies, sprites, or ghosts.

***Book of the Dun Cow*** *(c. 12th century)*
*cited in: Evans-Wentz, The Fairy-Faith in Celtic Countries (1911)*
> In the two chief Irish MSS., the Book of the Dun Cow and the Book of Leinster, the Tuatha De Danann are described as 'gods and not-gods'; and Sir John Rhys considers this an ancient formula comparable with the Sanskrit deva and adeva.
