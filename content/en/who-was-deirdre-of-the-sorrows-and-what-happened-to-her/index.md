Deirdre of the Sorrows was a tragic heroine from Irish mythology, specifically the Ulster Cycle, whose story brought destruction to Ulster through her love affair with Naoise, one of the sons of Usnech, ultimately leading to her death and the exile of prominent Ulster warriors.

## Key Facts
- Deirdre was born to Fedlimid, the storyteller of King Conchobar of Ulster, in a tale that begins in the house where Ulster men were drinking.
- She ranks as one of the great tragic heroines of literature, though less well known than her Greek counterparts.
- Her story explains how Fergus mac Roig, Dubtach, and other Ulster warriors joined the forces of Connacht against their native province.
- The tale connects to the Cattle-Raid of Cooley, where these exiled Ulster warriors assisted Ailill and Medb against Ulster.
- Deirdre's story became the theme of several Anglo-Irish dramas, notably J.M. Synge's "Deirdre of the Sorrows".

## Q&A Sections
### What was prophesied about Deirdre's birth?
The tale begins with Deirdre's birth in unusual circumstances, born to the wife of Fedlimid while the men of Ulster were gathered drinking in his house. The wife was "great with child" and stood attending the men before giving birth (sources limited).

### How did Deirdre's story lead to political consequences for Ulster?
Deirdre's tragic fate resulted in significant political upheaval for Ulster. The murder of the "sons of Usnech" prompted Fergus, Dubtach, and Cormac to seek revenge by slaying many of their fellow-tribesmen and departing Ulster with three thousand followers. These exiled warriors then joined the court of Ailill and Medb of Connacht, Ulster's traditional enemy, and assisted them in the Cattle-Raid of Cooley and other raids against Ulster.

### How did Deirdre die?
Deirdre met a violent end after being forced into an unbearable situation. King Conchobar declared that she would live with Eogan, son of Durthacht, for a year, giving her over into Eogan's hand. The next day, as they traveled across the fair-green of Macha with Deirdre standing behind Eogan in the chariot, she found herself between "the two men whom she had sworn never to see together upon the earth". When Conchobar taunted her, saying she shared her glance between them "the same glance that a ewe gives when between two rams," Deirdre struck her head against a great rock of stone in front of them, shattering her head and dying.

## In-Depth Overview
### Literary Significance and Modern Adaptations
Deirdre's story holds a prominent place in Irish literature as one of the great tragic narratives of the Ulster Cycle. Despite being less internationally known than Greek tragic heroines, she deserves recognition as a major literary figure whose story has resonated through the centuries. The tale gained renewed prominence in the modern period through theatrical adaptations, most notably J.M. Synge's drama "Deirdre of the Sorrows," which brought the ancient story to contemporary audiences.

The story also reportedly influenced visual arts during the Celtic Revival period, though the provided sources do not confirm the specific details of artistic depictions (sources limited).

### Political and Social Context
The Deirdre story serves multiple narrative functions beyond its tragic romantic elements. It provides an origin explanation for major political realignments within Ulster society, specifically accounting for why prominent Ulster warriors came to fight against their own people. The tale explains how the aftermath of Deirdre's story led to the exile of significant figures including Fergus mac Roig, Dubtach, and Conchobar's own son Cormac.

These political consequences had lasting ramifications, as the exiled warriors took three thousand followers with them to Connacht, where they became allies of Ailill and Medb. This defection provided crucial support to Ulster's enemies during major conflicts, including the famous Cattle-Raid of Cooley, one of the central narratives of the Ulster Cycle.

### The Tragic Ending
The conclusion of Deirdre's story represents one of the most brutal endings in Irish literature. After the deaths of the sons of Usnech, Deirdre found herself completely powerless, passed between men as property. Conchobar's decision to give her to Eogan for a year represented the final humiliation, forcing her to live with both men who had been central to her tragedy.

The imagery of her final moments is particularly stark - standing in a chariot between the two men "whom she had sworn never to see together upon the earth," she became the target of Conchobar's cruel taunting. His comparison of her to "a ewe gives when between two rams" reduced her to an animal caught between competing males, prompting her decisive and violent response. Her death by striking her head against a rock represents both suicide and a final act of agency in circumstances where she had been stripped of all other choices.
