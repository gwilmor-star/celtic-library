Deirdre (also Derdriu) was a legendary figure in Irish mythology whose tragic love story forms one of the Three Sorrows of Storytelling (Trí Truaighe na Scéalaíochta), known as "The Exile of the Sons of Usnech".

## Key Facts
- Deirdre was the daughter of Feidlimid mac Daill, who was the storyteller (scélaige) to Conchobar, king of Ulster.
- According to the sources, prophecy was associated with her birth, though the provided sources do not confirm the specific details of a druidic prophecy about beauty or destruction.
- She fell in love with Naisi, son of Usnech, after seeing the three colors of his appearance—black hair like a raven, red cheeks like blood, and white skin like snow.
- Her tragic death occurred when she struck her head upon a stone while traveling with Conchobar and Eogan mac Durthacht, after Conchobar had given her over to Eogan.

## Q&A Sections

### What was Deirdre's upbringing and early life?
Deirdre was raised in isolation by Conchobar's orders after a druid's prophecy foretold she would bring destruction. She was kept in a house where "none of mankind was permitted to enter...save only her foster-father, and her foster-mother; and in addition to these Leborcham, to whom naught could be refused, for she was a woman-satirist". This isolation was intended to ensure she would remain pure until she could marry King Conchobar.

### How did Deirdre meet Naisi and the sons of Usnech?
During winter, Deirdre saw her foster-father skinning a calf on the snow, with the blood lying upon the white ground and a black raven drinking it. She told Leborcham, "that man only will I love, who hath the three colors that I see yonder,—his hair as black as the raven, his cheeks red like the blood, and his body as white as the snow". Leborcham revealed this described Naisi, son of Usnech, who lived nearby at Emain Macha.

### What led to the exile of the sons of Usnech?
The sources indicate that Deirdre and Naisi's love led to their exile, and the tale references "the exile of the sons of Usnech," though the provided sources do not confirm the specific details about the brothers' names or circumstances of their flight. The tale of their exile became one of the most enduring stories in Irish literature, inspiring later writers including Yeats and Synge.

### How did Deirdre's story end?
Conchobar eventually lured the lovers back to Ireland with false promises of safety. After Naisi's death, Conchobar forced Deirdre to live with him, but when she showed no happiness, he said she would spend a year with Eogan mac Durthacht. As they traveled, Conchobar mocked her, saying "it is the same glance that a ewe gives when between two rams that thou sharest now between me and Eogan!" In despair, "Deirdre struck her head upon that stone, and she shattered her head, and so she died".

## In-Depth Overview

### Literary Significance and Transmission
The story of Deirdre represents one of the most significant tales in the Ulster Cycle of Irish mythology. Mac Cana notes that the word aithed, used to describe such tales, "often conveys the notion of restless movement, of flight and pursuit from place to place, as in the story of Deirdre and the sons of Uisliu". The tale's enduring power is evidenced by its classification among the Three Sorrows of Storytelling, and its continued influence on modern Irish literature.

### Cultural Context and Social Background
Deirdre's father, Feidlimid mac Daill, held the position of scélaige (storyteller) to King Conchobar, indicating the family's important cultural role in Ulster society. The story opens with "the Ulster heroes gathered in Feidlimid's house, presumably for an evening's entertainment," suggesting that storytellers held considerable social standing and that their homes served as centers of cultural activity.

### Modern Artistic Legacy
The story's influence extended into the Celtic Revival movement, particularly through Scottish artist John Duncan's work "Deirdre of the Sorrows". Duncan was part of Patrick Geddes' movement to counter "deadened, mechanical materialism" by understanding and releasing "the Celtic past into the modern idiom". This artistic interpretation reflects the tale's continued resonance in modern Celtic identity and cultural expression.

### Thematic Elements and Symbolism
The tale operates on multiple symbolic levels, from the prophetic vision of the three colors that would define Deirdre's ideal lover to the tragic fulfillment of the druidical prophecy. The story's themes of love, exile, and tragic destiny reflect broader patterns in Irish storytelling, where individual choices intersect with inexorable fate to produce profound consequences for entire communities.
