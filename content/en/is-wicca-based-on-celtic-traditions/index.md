Not in any direct historical way. Wicca was put together in England in the middle of the twentieth century. It does borrow some Celtic myths and festival names, but it pulls from many other places too, and it grew out of a modern mix of Romanticism and Western magic rather than from anything we can dig up or read about real ancient Celtic practice. Look closely, and the gap between Wicca and genuine Celtic religion turns out to be a wide one.

## 1. Is Wicca an ancient religion?

No: Wicca is a modern religion, not an ancient one. It took shape in England in the middle of the twentieth century, which is far too late to be a survival from the Celtic past.

In her encyclopaedia of Celtic mythology, the scholar Patricia Monaghan traces Wicca's "rituals and philosophy to mid-20th-century England. Celtic holidays are celebrated (as are the solar holidays of equinoxes and solstices), but contemporary Wicca is not a Celtic religion." (1)

Keeping festivals with Celtic names doesn't make a religion Celtic, any more than burning a Yule log makes Christmas Norse. And the more careful Wiccans know this themselves. As Monaghan notes, "more serious practitioners recognize that the rituals of contemporary witchcraft arose in the mid-20th century in England and represent an attempted reconstruction of European paganism." (2)

A reconstruction is something built fresh. It can use some old materials, but it is not the same as a thing that came down to us unbroken from the past. To see how Wicca was made, we need to look at the man who did most of the making.

## 2. Who created Wicca, and what idea was it built on?

Gerald Gardner created it in the 1940s and 1950s, on an idea that scholars have since rejected. That idea, popularised in his day, held that the people punished as witches in the Middle Ages had really belonged to a secret pagan religion that survived in hiding.

The scholar Angelika Heike Rüdiger lays out what Gardner did with that idea:

> "Gerald Gardner was a firm believer in Murray theory: indeed, he tried to create an alternative religion promoted as a revival of one long-suppressed. His faith, rooted in a mysterious past, elaborated in his Book of Shadows, became Wicca, the oldest variant — and one of the most important — of modern neo-pagan witchcraft." (3)

So what was the "Murray theory"? It was the claim, made by a historian named Margaret Murray, that the mediaeval witch trials had caught real members of an old pre-Christian fertility religion. Most scholars no longer accept this. So Wicca was built on ground that has since been pulled out from under it, and the further claim that it carries on something specifically Celtic has even less to stand on.

The historian Ronald Hutton points out that the wider idea of ancient pagan survival in Britain got another boost later on from Anne Ross, "someone who had been a front-rank scholar," (4) though she set aside careful method to make the case. When respected people are willing to back a claim, the public keeps believing it long after the evidence has fallen apart.

**Deeper reading:** Why did Gardner need his religion to be old in the first place? Because in the world he came from, old meant true. A faith that had supposedly survived in secret since before the Romans carried a weight no fresh invention could match. Antiquity, in other words, was doing the real work: it was the ground the whole thing stood on. Take the Murray thesis away, as scholars have, and there is nothing left underneath.

## 3. Does Wicca borrow anything from Celtic tradition?

Yes, it does borrow real Celtic material, so the answer isn't a flat no. But it picks and chooses, mixes its pickings with bits from everywhere else, and reshapes them to suit itself.
Philip Carr-Gomm, who writes from inside the modern Druid and Wiccan world, points to the *Carmina Gadelica*: a collection of Scottish Gaelic prayers gathered in the nineteenth century. He says it "has been one of the many sources of inspiration for both modern Wicca and Druidry," and that a common Wiccan self-blessing is "based on a blessing given to newborn children that was undoubtedly originally Druidic." (5)

Notice that phrase, "undoubtedly originally Druidic." It is a familiar move: claim a deep ancient origin, offer no proof. The prayers themselves are real and worth having. But they were written down in the nineteenth century and passed through an English Romantic filter before any Wiccan ever used them.

The goddess at the heart of Wicca tells the same story. Sharon Paice MacLeod describes how "the modern worship of this new Goddess archetype continues to grow at an exponential rate," how it is woven into Wicca and movements "whose origins date back to the late 19th and early 20th centuries," and how "early occultists blended European folklore with reports of witchcraft trials and Victorian perceptions about the ancient religions of Greece, Rome, Egypt and the Near East." (6)

So the Wiccan goddess is not a Celtic goddess. She is a patchwork, stitched together from many traditions, with a few Celtic threads in the mix.

**Deeper reading:** Look at what the two examples share. The translation of the *Carmina Gadelica* prayers and the dreaming of the great Wiccan goddess are both young: nineteenth and early-twentieth-century work, every bit of it. Yet both arrive labelled as ancient. That is the pattern worth watching. Wicca doesn't simply borrow Celtic things; it borrows recent things and ages them on the way in. The oldness is part of the appeal, so the oldness gets supplied, with or without the evidence to back it.

## 4. How are Wicca and modern Druidry related?

They are two separate modern movements that have grown closer by mixing with each other. Some of the Celtic flavour in today's Wicca actually arrived this way: borrowed from modern Druidry, which is itself a recent invention, not an unbroken line back to the ancient Celts.

Carr-Gomm watches the traffic going both ways. He notes that "many Wiccans have become interested in the history of the Druids, in Celtic myths, and in Druid animal and tree lore," while "many Druids have become interested in the more intuitive and magical approaches to life that are found in Wicca." (7)

This swapping is recent, and people chose to do it. It is not a sign of shared ancient roots.

Gardner's own dealings with Celtic Christianity show the same magpie habit. He "and [Ross] Nichols were both interested in the early British Church and became ordained in obscure unorthodox branches of Christianity: Gardner in the Ancient British Church in 1949, Nichols in the Ancient Celtic Church in 1963." (8)

Those labels, "Ancient British" and "Ancient Celtic," are wishful thinking dressed up as history. Calling your church ancient does not make it old.

## 5. Do any Wiccan groups have genuine Welsh or Irish roots?

Some groups say they do, claiming a secret Welsh or Irish tradition passed down unbroken. When you look closely, those claims don't hold up.

Rüdiger looked at these groups and found a simple problem. "It is obvious that these groups have no genuine knowledge of the Welsh language, which is strong evidence against their claims of secret, unbroken traditions." (9)

A culture's knowledge travels in its language. If a group claims deep Welsh roots but can't read a word of Welsh, then whatever it is drawing on, it isn't Welsh sources. It is leaning on English-language go-betweens, and many of those were themselves dreamed up during the Romantic revival.

One case makes the point well. There is a branch of Wicca called the "Faerie Faith," which "uses Graves' tree alphabet, another poetical invention based on medieval Welsh poetry, whose name suggests the influence of Evans-Wentz." (10) That tree alphabet comes from Robert Graves's book *The White Goddess*, published in 1948. It is a poet's invention from the twentieth century, not an ancient system anyone has found in the records. Using it as a Celtic ritual frame is a perfect snapshot of the whole problem: someone's modern creativity gets mistaken for an ancient survival.

**Deeper reading:** There is a quiet test running under this whole question, and it is language. The real Celtic past lives inside old tongues: Middle Welsh, Old Irish, Latin. It takes years of work to read a single line of any of them. So there are really two doors into "Celtic tradition." One goes through the languages to the sources themselves. The other goes through English books, a good many of them Victorian inventions. Wicca, for all its Celtic colour, nearly always comes through the second door. A group that cannot read Welsh or Irish isn't guarding a Welsh secret; it is retelling an English story about one.

## 6. What did real Celtic religion actually look like?

Rooted in real texts and specific to real places. Some fossilised remains of the myths are there, but reading and understanding them takes training.

Take the goddess Brigit. A ninth-century Irish text called Cormac's Glossary describes her like this:

> "It is therefore they call her goddess of poets by this name. Whose sisters were Brigit the female physician, Brigit the female smith; from whose names with all Irishmen a goddess was called Brigit." (11)

So here is a goddess in three parts: poetry, healing, and metalwork, all at once. She belongs to a particular learned tradition, she has particular jobs, and she is described in a particular language. She is not one aspect of some universal "sacred feminine." She is a sharply drawn Irish goddess, and you need training in Old Irish to read her description in the original.

The same goes for the offerings people left at holy wells, a habit found across the Celtic lands from the late Bronze Age on. MacLeod calls the practice "one of the most ancient and widespread in the entire tradition," one that survived "throughout the centuries in spite of political turmoil and religious upheaval." (12)

This is a real practice we can look at and date. But MacLeod points to a snag: "many of these sources are difficult for students to access or understand, and this has led to the fabrication of inaccurate or misleading perceptions about Celtic culture and beliefs." (13) When the real sources are locked behind hard languages and years of study, the empty space gets filled with invention.

**Deeper reading:** Look again at how the Irish drew Brigit: poet, healer, and smith all at once, with named sisters and a craft in each hand. That exactness is the main difference. The goddess of modern Wicca is wide and open, a figure who can incorporate almost anyone. Brigit is not of that nature. She is tied too tightly to particular skills, a particular language, and the particular learned people who kept her name alive. The old gods resist being boiled down into one all-purpose goddess, and they resist it because the people who wrote them down cared about the details of who they were.

## 7. If Wicca isn't ancient Celtic religion, what is it?

It is a modern religion, one of a family of new spiritual movements that grew out of Romanticism and the nineteenth-century fashion for all things Celtic. That makes it real and viable for many people, but it does not make it old.

The Celtic revival it sprang from has stirred up real cultural energy, and Monaghan is fair about it: "some of the material they believe traditional is of relatively recent origin; some of their rituals derive from Masonic rather than Celtic sources; some of their hopeful social attitudes are not yet supported by archaeology. Yet Celtic revivalism seems unlikely to wane in the near future." (14)

Hutton sets Wicca's founders in their right place. They "were less the representatives of an age-old peasant resistance movement and more a trained group of religious and magical practitioners, answering a growing call from mainstream society. They were no longer so much the inheritors of an archaic faith as members of the earliest, and foremost, of an important constellation of modern successors." (15)

That group is real, and the needs it answers are real too. But a new constellation is not an old one, no matter how brightly it burns.

So, to sum up: Wicca is a twentieth-century creation that borrows Celtic names, festivals, and images and folds them into a wider mix of Western magic, Romanticism, and spiritual searching. The real record of Celtic religion, the texts and the things in the ground, is richer, stranger, and far harder to read than most modern pagan practice lets on. Both can be true at the same time: Wicca may give its followers something they truly value, and it may still have almost nothing to do with the religion of the ancient Celts.
