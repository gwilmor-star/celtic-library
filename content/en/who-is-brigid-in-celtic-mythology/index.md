Brigid is one of the best-recorded deities in the Celtic world. Her name means "the exalted one," and she oversees poetry, healing, metalwork, and kingship. We find her in the oldest Irish myths, she leaves her mark on place-names and tribal names across Britain and Europe, and shows up again as Saint Brigit of Kildare, whose feast day on 1 February still marks the start of spring. Where does the goddess stop and the saint begin? Scholars have argued about that for generations, and the question is still open.

## 1. What does the name Brigid mean and how far did she spread?

Brigid's name isn't really a name at all, but a title meaning "the exalted one," and it crops up across the Celtic world and beyond. The same word and the same idea reach from Ireland to northern Britain and all the way back to ancient India, which tells you how old and how widespread this figure was. As Mac Cana says:

> "The name Brighid was originally an epithet meaning 'the exalted one', just as its cognate brihati was used as a divine epithet in Vedic Sanskrit, and this perhaps gives point to Cormac's remark quoted above that 'among all the Irish a goddess used to be called Brighid'. It had a close correspondent in the British Briganti, latinised as Brigantia, 'the exalted one', tutelary goddess of the Brigantes." (1)

So "Brigit" may never have been a personal name in the way we usually mean. It was more like a job description. And it has deep roots: the Sanskrit *bṛhatī*, the Romano-British Brigantia, the tribal name of the Brigantes in northern Britain, and maybe even the Welsh *brenin* ("king," from *Brigantīnos*) all grow from the same root. That root carries the idea of being raised high in a way that's tied up with kingship. The name even runs in the rivers. The Brent in England and the Braint in Wales both seem to come from Brigantia, and in Celtic tradition a river-goddess could mark out the territory of the people who worshiped her. (1)

## 2. Why is Brigid called a triple goddess?

Brigid is called a triple goddess because the oldest Irish source describes her as three sisters, all named Brigit, each in charge of a different skill: poetry, healing, and metalwork. Tripling a god was a common Celtic way of saying "this one is especially powerful," so the three Brigits are best understood as one great figure shown three times over.

That oldest Irish source is *Sanas Chormaic*, a ninth-century word-list put together under Cormac mac Cuilennáin, the Bishop-King of Munster. Its description is short but the shape of it is striking:

> "It is therefore they call her goddess of poets by this name. Whose sisters were Brigit the female physician [woman of leechcraft,] Brigit the female smith [woman of smithwork]; from whose names with all Irishmen a goddess was called Brigit." (2)

Three sisters, all called Brigit, each minding one of the skills a community can't do without: the word, the healing, and the forge. The usual reading follows naturally: "a common theory drawn from this text, ... is that Brigid was a triple goddess; one goddess with three personalities that each had a certain purpose; a goddess of poetry/wisdom, medicine, and metalwork." (3) Was this one goddess split into three, or three separate figures who slowly merged under one name? That's harder to know. But the glossary is clear that the name Brigit covered every goddess in Ireland. "This triplication, a frequent occurrence among the Celts, typically emphasized or intensified a figure's divine power," (4) and with Brigid the result was a goddess who seemed to have a hand in just about everything.

**Deeper reading:** Notice what the three skills have in common. Poetry, healing, and metalwork aren't a random list, but three crafts that turn a band of people into a settled, working society. The poet keeps the memory, the law, and the praise that holds a people together; the healer keeps them alive; the smith makes the tools and the weapons. When the tradition gathered exactly these three under one name and called her "the exalted one," it was a sacred statement that civilisation itself rests on skilled making. Brigid isn't the goddess of one craft. She's the goddess of the very idea that human skill builds the civilised world.

## 3. What happens to Brigid in the Second Battle of Mag Tuired?

In this great myth Brigid (here called Bríg) loses her son Ruadán in battle and weeps over his body; the Irish remembered her as the one who keened for the dead before anyone else ever had. Her grief is the first mourning-cry in Ireland, which makes her the inventor of a custom that outlived the war she was caught up in.

The Second Battle of Mag Tuired is the mythological clash between the god-like Túatha Dé Danann and the monstrous Fomoire. Brigid stands right on the border between the two: she's the daughter of the Dagda, a chief of the Túatha Dé, but also the wife of Bres, the Fomorian king. Her son Ruadán has blood from both sides. He's sent to spy on the Túatha Dé's smiths and then to kill Goibniu, their master craftsman. He fails, and is killed himself. Then:

> "Bríg came and keened for her son. At first she shrieked, in the end she wept. Then for the first time weeping and shrieking were heard in Ireland. (Now she is the Bríg who invented a whistle for signalling at night.)" (5)

This has the flat, matter-of-fact tone of an origin story: keening, the ritual wailing for the dead that stayed at the heart of Irish mourning for centuries, was invented by a goddess crying over her own child. The line about the night-whistle is harder to explain, but it's making the same kind of claim, crediting Brigid with a practical invention right alongside the emotional one. And because she's the Dagda's daughter *and* Bres's wife, her grief is in an awkward place. She mourns at the exact spot where the gods and their enemies touched, and her lament starts something that will outlast the battle.

**Deeper reading:** Notice where the tradition chose to place the very first act of mourning. Not in the camp of the gods, and not among their monstrous enemies, but in the one figure who belongs to both at once: daughter of one side, wife and mother on the other. Grief, the story seems to say, is the thing that's shared regardless, from the place where the two worlds are joined by a single body. And the same goddess who looks after poetry is the one who turns the first raw shriek into weeping. That little shift, from shriek to weep, is the moment a sound becomes a form. It's the poet's work coming to the graveside.

## 4. Who is Brigantia and how is she linked to the Roman goddess Minerva?

Brigantia might be the same or similar exalted goddess worshipped in northern Britain, where she was the patron deity of a tribe called the Brigantes and may have stood for queenship and rule. When the Romans arrived they matched her with their own goddess Minerva, because both were tied to craft, wisdom, and war, though that tidy match hides real differences.

"Her role in Northern Britain as Brigantia is one of 'High One' or 'Queen' of the Brigantes. Here, she is worshipped by a 'strong hegemony of tribes,' whose ultimate unity is due to this powerful goddess... Brigit's earthly representatives, the Queens of the Brigantes, were bestowed with her power and in a sense, women such as Cartimandua and Boudicca, were a temporal reflection of the goddess." (6) What the name hints at is a goddess of rule embodied in actual queens that drew their right to rule from her.

Roman soldiers posted near Hadrian's Wall left dedications to Brigantia that treat her as Minerva, and that sets off a chain of guesses: "we have a pattern of equating connections: Celtic goddess Brigid → Celtic goddess Brigantia → Roman goddess Minerva. In fact, Brigit herself is also commonly equated with Minerva; Brigantia is not considered necessary to make the connection between the two." (7) The *interpretatio romana* (the Roman habit of renaming foreign gods after whichever of their own they most resembled) is a  clue to what a god *did*, but a bad guide to who that god really *was*. Brigid and Minerva do share craft, wisdom, and war. But the match irons out differences the original worshippers might have thought were native and unique.

## 5. Why is Saint Brigid's day on 1 February and was Brigid a fire goddess?

Saint Brigid's day falls on 1 February, probably because it also lands on Imbolc, the old festival marking the first stirrings of spring, when ewes start to give milk. The link to fire is shakier: there was a famous sacred fire at her shrine in Kildare, but we can't be sure it goes back to a pagan fire-goddess rather than developing later.

The tie to fertility and the return of the grazing season is ancient and steady. Fire is the messier part of the story. Giraldus Cambrensis, writing in the twelfth century, described a remarkable sacred enclosure at Kildare:

> "FIRE IS SURROUNDED by a hedge which is circular and made of withies, and which no male may cross. And if by chance one does dare to enter – and some rash people have at times tried it – he does not escape the divine vengeance. Only women are allowed to blow the fire, and then not with the breath of their mouths, but only with bellows or winnowing forks." (8)

This is strong evidence that there really was a fire cult at Kildare in Giraldus's own day. The question is how far back it reaches. Hutton points out that "in legend, however, the goddess was not especially associated with fire, and (more significantly) the very important seventh-century life of St Brigid written by Cogitosus at Kildare makes no mention of the custom; it is possible, therefore, that it grew up between the seventh and twelfth centuries as a derivation from the meaning of the saint's name." (9) There's a real trap here: "there is a danger of creating a picture of her pagan role from the information we have of Brigit as a saint, because certain elements of her life as a Christian holy woman appear to be pre-Christian in origin. An example of this is the saint's magical association with fire, which has given rise to the deity being identified as a fire-goddess." (10) So we should be cautious with the "fire-goddess" label. The fire at Kildare is real history. How old it is, and whether it connects to anything pagan, we simply don't know.

**Deeper reading:** Brigid's name links to brightness and height, and brightness suggests flame, so a sacred fire at her shrine looks, at first glance, like proof she was always a fire-goddess. But the evidence runs the other way round. The fire is recorded late; the early sources are quiet about it. What we may be seeing isn't an ancient pagan flame surviving into Christian times, but a meaning hidden inside a word slowly turning itself into a ritual. The name, perhaps, came first, and the fire may have caught from it.

## 6. Was Saint Brigit of Kildare originally the goddess Brigid?

We can't prove it definitively, but the saint and the goddess might share far more than a name. Saint Brigit of Kildare carries fire that doesn't burn, brews ale, works love-magic, and ends up with the title "Mary of the Gael," and if Cormac was correct, it echoes the old claim that every goddess in Ireland was called Brigit.

Saint Brigit founded the great monastery at Kildare, and her saint's Life reads like more than coincidence. The *Bethu Brigte* opens with a scene packed with meaning:

> "they saw that the house had caught fire behind them. The people run to its aid, thinking that they would not find one house-post against another. The house is found intact and the girl asleep ... On a certain day the druid was asleep and he saw three clerics wearing white hooded garments baptizing (the above-mentioned) Brigit, and one of the three said to him, 'Let Brigit be your name for the girl.'" (11)

Fire that doesn't burn the house down; a pagan druid dreaming of Christian clergy naming the child after Cormac's triple goddess, the author here isn't really hiding the older echoes. The saint's later miracles keep the pattern: she performs "many acts of generosity and abundance, as well as the copious brewing of ale and the performing of love magic," (12) which sit far more comfortably beside a fertility goddess than beside most early medieval saints. And the close of the *Betha Brigte* gives the saint a title of almost impossible size:

> "She is that helpeth every one who is in straits and in danger. She it is that abateth the pestilences. She it is that quelleth the wave-voice and the wrath of the great sea. This is the prophesied woman of Christ. She is the Queen of the South. She is the Mary of the Gael." (13)

"Mary of the Gael": the saint has soaked up some of that total, goddess-of-everything quality that *Sanas Chormaic* gave her when it said all the goddesses in Ireland went by the name Brigit.

**Deeper reading:** The glossary recorded one name, Brigit, that came to stand for every goddess in Ireland. The saint's Life calls her "Mary of the Gael": one woman who stands for the whole nation's faith. The shape is the same in both: a single figure encompassing everything, becoming the name under which all the rest are gathered. Whatever the historians can or can't prove about a direct line from goddess to saint, the Irish kept reaching for the same pattern. They wanted a Brigit who was not one thing among many, but the one that contained the many.

## 7. Can we prove the goddess Brigid became Saint Brigit?

No, the surviving sources won't let us prove it either way. The overlap of name, date, and skills is too exact to be pure chance, but every text we have was written down by Christians centuries later, so the direction and the mechanism of the link can't be pinned down.

The trouble starts with the sources themselves. "There are no contemporary written accounts of the goddess Brigid as she flourished in the period before the introduction of writing to Ireland. It is, however, possible to deduce aspects of her function and her attributes from a number of other sources of information, particularly from the Celtic goddess Brigantia, from Brigantia's supposed Roman equivalent Minerva, and from St Brigid herself." (14) Every account we've got has been passed through centuries of Christian Latin learning, so the goddess has to be reconstructed by working backwards from later clues.

And scholars are still arguing about it: "although it is still a matter of controversy among scholars, some argue that the saint is connected to the pre-Christian Celtic divinity also named Brigid, who was seen as a protector of her people and who was associated with the sun and light. In this view, legends of St. Brigid would have attributed to her some of the features of the earlier goddess, which would have enhanced the saint's reputation even though at the same time she is represented as distinctively Christian." (15)

The way name, date, and role agree across goddess and saint is too close to wave away as coincidence, but you can't get the exact path of influence, or even its direction, out of the texts that have come down to us. What we *can* say with confidence is that a goddess called "the exalted one," patron of the skills that make a society possible, whose reach ran from Munster to the Brigantes' land in northern Britain and beyond, sat near the centre of pre-Christian Celtic thinking, and the memory of her shaped, in ways we can follow but never fully measure, one of medieval Ireland's most loved saints.
