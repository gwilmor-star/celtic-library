The Táin Bó Cúailnge is Ireland's greatest epic tale, recounting Queen Medb of Connacht's cattle raid to capture the Brown Bull of Cooley from Ulster, defended single-handedly by the hero Cú Chulainn while the Ulster warriors suffer from a debilitating curse.

## Key Facts

- The Táin tells of an invasion of Ulster by Queen Medb and King Ailill of Connacht to seize the Brown Bull from the Cooley peninsula.
- Cú Chulainn defends Ulster alone while the men of Ulster are afflicted by a curse that leaves them temporarily disabled.
- The tale exists in two main recensions, compiled from material dating from the ninth century or earlier through the twelfth century.
- The epic culminates in single combat between the two great bulls, Donn Cúailnge and Finnbhennach.
- The language of the earliest form dates to the eighth century, though some verse passages may be two centuries older.

## Q&A Sections

### What motivates Queen Medb to launch the cattle raid?
Queen Medb instigates the raid to capture the Brown Bull of Cooley (Donn Cúailnge) from Ulster. The tale begins when Medb and her husband Ailill compare their possessions and discover that Ailill owns the superior White-Horned Bull (Finnbhennach), while Medb lacks an equivalent. To remedy this imbalance, Medb assembles a great army from Connacht and allied provinces to seize Ulster's Brown Bull by force.

### Why can't the Ulster warriors defend their territory initially?
The Ulstermen suffer from a debilitating curse that prevents them from defending their province for three months during winter. This supernatural affliction, known as the "Pangs of Ulster," leaves the warriors temporarily incapacitated. The curse creates an asymmetrical conflict where "we begin with the muster of the Connachta and their allies, and we do not reach the muster of the Ulstermen until more than three-fourths of the tale has been told".

### How does Cú Chulainn defend Ulster single-handedly?
Cú Chulainn engages in single combat at fords to delay the invading army, following the heroic convention where "the temporary disablement of the men of Ulster requires (and allows)" his individual defense. He vanquishes numerous opponents in formal duels, including his beloved foster-brother Fer Diad in one of the tale's most dramatic episodes. Even when the invaders capture the bull, "Cú Chulainn remains an obstacle in their path" until the Ulster warriors recover from their affliction.

### What is the significance of the battle between the two bulls?
The climactic bull-fight between Donn Cúailnge (Brown Bull of Cooley) and Finnbhennach (White-Horned Bull) represents the ultimate resolution of the conflict that began the entire cattle raid. After Medb brings Donn Cúailnge back to Connacht, "the bull fights Finnbhennach, kills him, but is mortally wounded". The wounded Brown Bull then wanders around Ireland, dropping pieces of his defeated opponent from his horns and creating placenames before returning home to die of exhaustion.

### How does the Táin end?
The tale concludes with the recovery of the Ulster warriors from their curse and their decisive victory in a great battle against the Connacht forces. Following the death of both bulls, "Ailill and Medb made peace with the Ulstermen and with Cú Chulainn. For seven years after there was no wounding of men between them". The Connacht army returns home while "the Ulstermen to Emain Macha with their great triumph".

## In-Depth Overview

### Historical Context and Dating
The Táin Bó Cúailnge represents one of Ireland's most important medieval literary achievements, with its origins reaching deep into the oral tradition. The language of the earliest recorded form dates to the eighth century, though some verse passages may be two centuries older, and most Celtic scholars believe the Ulster cycle had "a long oral existence before it received a literary shape" at the hands of monastic scribes. The tale exists in two main recensions: the first compiled from material ranging from the ninth century or earlier to the eleventh century, and a second recension from the twelfth century.

### Literary Structure and Genre
The Táin functions as Ireland's national epic, comparable in scope and significance to classical works. As one scholar notes, combining "prose and verse, the Táin meets the criteria set out by Abrams: it is a long narrative work, related for the most part in an elevated style; its subject matter, the invasion and defence of Ulster, is great and serious; it is centered on the heroic figure of Cú Chulainn". The work demonstrates remarkable complexity beneath its simple outline, being "single-minded in the achievement of its purpose, but multifarious in the means so used".

### The Ulster Warriors' Affliction
Central to the tale's dramatic structure is the supernatural curse affecting the Ulster warriors, creating the conditions that require Cú Chulainn's single-handed defense. The curse manifests as a debilitating condition that strikes during the winter months when Medb launches her invasion. This affliction creates what scholars term an "asymmetrical" warfare narrative, where the defending province cannot initially muster its full military strength. The supernatural element reflects deeper mythological themes while providing narrative justification for the hero's extraordinary role.

### Legal and Social Framework
The narrative is "rich in legal vocabulary" that appears prominently in both recensions and reflects early Irish legal concepts. Legal language is already evident in one of the tale's precursors, a seventh-century poem attributed to Luccreth Moccu Chíara, which "gives us a side-glimpse into the emotional and political background to Táin Bó Cúailnge". The opening line of this early poem, "Conailla Medb míchura" (Medb enjoined evil contracts), immediately establishes the legal dimension that permeates the larger narrative.

### Manuscript Tradition and Textual History
The Táin survives in multiple manuscript versions, with the earliest preserved in Lebor na hUidre (the Book of the Dun Cow) from the twelfth century, which contains "part of the earliest known form of the Táin Bó Cúailnge". Another partial version appears in the late fourteenth-century Yellow Book of Lecan. These manuscripts, while flawed and mutilated, provide the foundation for modern understanding of the text. The tale represents "the work of many hands" across several centuries of transmission.

### Mythological and Religious Dimensions
The narrative incorporates complex mythological elements that reflect pre-Christian Irish religious concepts while receiving "a few traces of Christian colour" from monastic scribes. The supernatural elements include not only the Ulster warriors' curse but also Cú Chulainn's extraordinary combat abilities and the magical nature of the bulls themselves. These mythic components are "woven into a literary work which is simple in its general outline, but remarkably complex in its detail".
