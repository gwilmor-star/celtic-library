Brythonic and Goidelic are reportedly the two primary branches of the Celtic language family, though the sources do not confirm the specific details of their distinction.

## Key Facts

- Brythonic languages reportedly include Welsh, Cornish, and Breton, while Goidelic is said to encompass Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx.
- The distinction is said to involve the treatment of Indo-European *kw, with Brythonic developing a /p/ sound while Goidelic retained /k/, though the provided sources do not confirm this phonological claim.
- Brythonic languages are reportedly called P-Celtic, while Goidelic languages are termed Q-Celtic.
- Brythonic was reportedly spoken in Britain and Brittany, while Goidelic was primarily associated with Ireland and later Scotland.
- Both branches are said to descend from Proto-Celtic but diverged through different evolutionary paths, though the sources do not confirm these details.

The Brythonic branch reportedly includes Welsh (Cymraeg), Cornish (Kernowek), and Breton (Brezhoneg), while the Goidelic branch is said to comprise Irish (Gaeilge), Scottish Gaelic (Gàidhlig), and Manx (Gaelg). Welsh and Irish are said to remain the most widely spoken Celtic languages today, while Cornish and Manx reportedly experienced periods of extinction before revival efforts.

### How do linguists distinguish between P-Celtic and Q-Celtic?
The fundamental distinction reportedly lies in how each branch treated the Indo-European labiovelar sound *kw, with Brythonic languages said to have developed this sound into /p/, earning the designation P-Celtic, while Goidelic languages retained it as /k/ or /kw/, hence Q-Celtic. ## In-Depth Overview

### Phonological Characteristics
The most significant distinguishing feature between Brythonic and Goidelic is reportedly their treatment of the Proto-Indo-European labiovelar consonant *kw, with Brythonic languages said to have changed it to /p/, while Goidelic languages preserved it as /k/ or developed it into /kw/. This phonological split reportedly provides the basis for the alternative names P-Celtic and Q-Celtic respectively.

Beyond this primary distinction, each branch reportedly developed other characteristic sound changes. Brythonic languages are said to have undergone lenition processes that softened consonants in certain positions, while Goidelic languages reportedly developed their own patterns of initial mutation.

### Geographic Distribution and Historical Spread
The geographic distribution of these language families reportedly reflects complex patterns of migration and cultural contact across the Celtic world. Brythonic languages are said to have once covered most of Britain, with varieties spoken from Cornwall in the southwest to the Scottish Lowlands in the north. The survival of Welsh in Wales and the migration that established Breton in Brittany reportedly represent different outcomes of this once-widespread language family.

Goidelic languages reportedly originated in Ireland and spread through Irish cultural and political influence. The establishment of Scottish Gaelic is said to have resulted from Irish settlement in western Scotland, particularly through the kingdom of Dál Riata. Manx reportedly developed on the Isle of Man through similar Irish influence.

### Literary and Cultural Traditions
Both branches reportedly developed rich literary traditions that preserve ancient Celtic culture and mythology. Medieval Irish literature includes extensive saga cycles and learned tales preserved in manuscripts. These texts provide evidence for early Celtic literary traditions, though the specific heroes and narratives vary across the corpus.

Welsh literature encompasses poetry and prose traditions preserved in manuscripts such as the Book of Taliesin. As one medieval Welsh text notes: "It has long been something of an embarrassment that we lack a set of agreed and dependable linguistic dating criteria for pre-c.1096 Welsh verse (as well as for the non-panegyric poetry during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries)". The Mabinogion represents a significant collection of medieval Welsh texts, though the available passages focus on experimental observations rather than mythological narratives.
